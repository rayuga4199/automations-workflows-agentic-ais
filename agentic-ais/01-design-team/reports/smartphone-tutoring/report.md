## 3. Gaps and Risks

**The coordinator is a single point of failure and the design does not fully protect against it.** The team resolved volunteer redundancy correctly with the primary-plus-backup tutor model. They did not apply the same logic to the coordinator. Every back-stage process runs through one unnamed person. The Coordinator Toolkit describes what that person must do; it does not describe what happens when they leave, are ill for two weeks, or are overloaded. This is the highest-probability failure mode in this type of service. It needs a named deputy role and a handover pack.

**The GDPR and DPA workflow is described but not designed.** Castellane flags a shared spreadsheet with DPA as the data handling solution. Okafor lists minimal GDPR workflows as a key gap. Aldaine's blueprint does not include a data processing stage or a consent touchpoint. In a UK library context operating with older adults, this is not a detail to defer. The client needs either a designed consent moment in the booking flow or a written statement that they have existing library data governance that covers this programme.

**Primary research has not happened yet.** Okafor's plan is a plan, not completed work. The design is built on secondary research and professional judgment. Several decisions are described as if validated. The word "evidence" should not appear in the client document without being qualified as secondary evidence or professional judgment.

**The volunteer onboarding flow is thorough but its delivery logistics are unspecified.** The training session is scripted but it is not clear who runs it, where, whether it is synchronous or asynchronous, or what happens when a new volunteer joins mid-programme. A 39% attrition rate means mid-programme volunteer intake is not an edge case. This needs a one-page logistics note.

**The exit stage of the service is underweight.** The Exit stage is the least specified. What happens after a learner achieves their goal is unclear. A route for learners to become volunteers themselves — a pattern that has been shown to strengthen sustainability — is absent.

**Success signals are defined but measurement is not.** The three signals are clean and appropriate. There is no designed mechanism to capture them. The coordinator needs to know what to record, how, and how often.

# SECONDARY RESEARCH REPORT: Community Library Smartphone Learning Programme for Older Adults

**Research conducted:** 30 October 2026
**Scope:** Digital literacy services for adults 65+, volunteer management models, UK context

---

## 1. What the Evidence Says

### Existing Programmes and Models

The UK has established digital inclusion infrastructure led by Good Things Foundation, which coordinates the National Digital Inclusion Network and reports that approximately **8.5 million UK adults lack basic digital skills** (Good Things Foundation, 2024). Age UK operates the Digital Champion Programme (2022–2026), which has trained 742 volunteer Digital Champions and supported 10,321 older people with digital skills sessions. The programme explicitly identifies barriers: around **69% of people aged 75+ struggle with basic online tasks**, and obstacles include device cost, internet access, security concerns, and lack of confidence (Age UK, 2024).

Norfolk Library and Information Service delivered 392 digital literacy support sessions (Feb 2023–Mar 2024) through both drop-in and bookable 1-on-1 appointments with library staff, trialling small group sessions (2–3 participants) as effective structures. Age UK delivers sessions from library locations (e.g., Ealing Central Library, Tuesdays 10:15am–4:15pm), combining one-on-one guidance, group awareness activities, device-loan schemes, and individual goal-focused sessions.

**Session structure research**: Effective programmes use protocolized sessions based on pre-assessed participant needs. Research from 2024 shows interventionists collaborate over 4–6 in-home sessions to identify priorities and co-develop personalized strategies around real-life goals (video calling family, booking GP appointments, safe shopping). **First sessions are structurally distinct**: initial sessions run approximately **3 hours with hands-on training**, followed by monthly or weekly sessions of 1–2 hours with reinforcement and repetition as core pedagogical strategies. Programmes that included at-home training, ongoing phone/email support, and reinforcement of key steps showed marked effectiveness.

---

### Barriers Older Adults Face

**Emotional and psychological**: Research shows older adults experience technology anxiety rooted in beliefs that "I cannot master it," triggering avoidance. **Heightened anxiety about making mistakes or compromising personal information leads to technology avoidance** — even when interested. Public failure and security concerns are significant emotional barriers.

**Cognitive and physical**: Older adults experience age-related cognitive changes, sensory impairments (vision, hearing), and motor difficulties maintaining prolonged smartphone use. Cognitive difficulties are reported in 28.3% of accessibility barriers, and physical difficulties in 42.5%.

**Environmental**: Noisy shared library spaces compound cognitive overload; device heterogeneity (iOS/Android) causes script ambiguity if tutors lack flexibility.

---

### Volunteer Management and Attrition

**Current crisis**: Volunteer attrition increased from 36% (2024) to 39% (2025). Virtual "one-and-done" opportunities show higher attrition; 20% of volunteers account for 80% of impact hours in most programmes. Only 44% of UK volunteer programmes have invested in management systems, despite technology being essential for tracking, reminding, and scheduling.

**Retention levers**: Technology platforms can increase recruitment but are less effective for retention unless they automate reminders, track milestones, and simplify scheduling. Organisations using mobile-first volunteer management systems see better retention; those that haven't gone mobile-first are losing volunteers.

**Training and confidence**: Age UK's Digital Champion volunteers receive structured training and ongoing support to provide one-on-one guidance. The frame's principle — design for volunteer confidence — aligns with evidence that trained, supported volunteers sustain programmes better than untrained enthusiasts.

---

### Key Touchpoints in Community Programmes

**Booking and reminders**: Multiple registration channels (phone, postal, SMS, email) accommodate varying digital comfort levels; printed invitations and follow-up calls are particularly important for those with limited digital skills.

**First session**: The first session is an inflection point for dropout risk; it must establish psychological safety and signal patient support. Pre-session touch points (reminder calls, explicit permission to ask for help, device arrangements) matter.

**Follow-up**: Community programmes with planned follow-ups (home visits, nurse-initiated calls in first month, then monthly phone/home visits) showed higher motivation and retention.

---

## 2. What Changed Recently (2024–2026)

- **Policy shift (2025)**: UK Government launched 80 free local digital skills training schemes across the country, signalling new funding and infrastructure for digital inclusion in libraries and community settings.

- **Accessibility research**: 2024–2025 research extends WCAG with age-specific design checklists; WCAG alone lacks provisions for cognitive load reduction critical for older users.

- **Volunteer crisis**: Attrition rose sharply 2024–2025 (36% → 39%), driven by low barrier opportunities ("one-and-done" tasks) and lack of sustained engagement systems.

- **Peer-to-peer models emerging**: Research into peer-to-peer digital education for older adults is now active as a clinical trial, suggesting interest in volunteer-peer models beyond formal tutoring.

---

## 3. What We Do Not Know

- **UK library-specific attrition**: No evidence on first-session dropout rates in UK library-based digital literacy (the frame notes this as a critical risk). Norfolk's report mentions session volume but not completion/continuation rates.

- **Volunteer cost and feasibility ceiling**: The frame names "volunteer ceiling" as a constraint; no data on how many tutors a single coordinator can sustain, or at what session volume quality degrades.

- **Device heterogeneity in tutoring practice**: While device variation is identified as a risk, no evidence on how tutors actually adapt scripts or whether structured branching by device (iOS/Android) improves confidence and outcomes.

- **Coordinator workload and role separation**: The frame identifies the coordinator as a single point of failure. No systematic data on task distribution, decision-making load, or risk-mitigation models (e.g., co-coordinators, tiered volunteer responsibility).

- **Session-one specificity**: Evidence shows first sessions are longer (~3 hours) and goal-focused, but no data specific to UK library contexts on dropout between session one and two, emotional safety signals, or what makes session-one tutors effective.

- **GDPR compliance in low-budget programmes**: Good guidance exists, but no evidence on minimal viable GDPR workflows for small volunteer programmes with tight budgets.

## 4. Primary Research Plan

### Objective
Validate session structures, volunteer readiness, first-contact dropout risk, and coordinator sustainability before launch.

### Recruitment and Sample

**Participant groups:**
1. **Older adults (8–10 participants)**: Aged 65+, little/no smartphone experience, varying confidence levels and physical/cognitive ability. Recruit by behaviour (e.g., "has not made a video call on phone" or "tried to use banking app and stopped"), not demographics alone. Sites: libraries, Age UK drop-in centres, GP waiting rooms.
2. **Volunteer tutors (4–5 participants)**: Existing Age UK Digital Champions or similar background (trained, some teaching exposure); recruit 2–3 new volunteers with no prior formal training to test onboarding.
3. **Library coordinators (2 participants)**: From different library services (small, medium); assess single-point-of-failure risk and decision-load.

### Method: Observation and Semi-Structured Interviews

**With older adults (4–5 one-on-one interviews, ~60 min each):**
1. "Tell me about a time you tried to learn something new on a phone. What happened? What did you feel at the moment?"
2. "What worries you most about making mistakes on a phone in front of someone else?"
3. "What's one concrete thing you want to do on your phone right now? Why does that matter to you?"
4. "If you were learning this, would you prefer 1-on-1 help, a small group, or something else? Why?"
5. "What would make you stop coming to sessions? What would keep you coming?"

**With volunteer tutors (2–3 structured interviews, ~45 min each):**
1. "Describe a moment when you felt unsure how to explain something to an older person. What did you do?"
2. "If a learner has an Android phone and you trained on iPhone, how do you handle that?"
3. "What would make you stop volunteering? What would keep you committed?"
4. "What do you think should happen in the very first session with a new learner?"
5. "What training or resources would help you feel ready to teach?"

**With coordinators (structured interviews, ~60 min each):**
1. "Walk me through booking one new volunteer, one new learner, and scheduling their first session. Where do you get stuck?"
2. "What's the riskiest part of running this right now?"
3. "On a typical week, what decisions do you make about sessions, volunteers, or learners that you can't delegate?"
4. "How do you know if someone's about to drop out? What signals do you see?"
5. "If you doubled the number of learners, what breaks first?"

### Observation Sessions

- **1–2 simulated first sessions** between trained volunteer and older adult (with consent; observed): Note emotional moments, where tutors deviate from script, device-branching struggles, session pacing.

### Data Outputs for Service Design

- **Session-one prototype**: emotional safety signals, goal-elicitation script, physical accessibility checklist.
- **Volunteer onboarding checklist**: training topics, confidence-building markers, device-variation branching.
- **Coordinator workflow map**: critical decisions, bottlenecks, single-point-of-failure mitigation.
- **Dropout risk indicators**: what signals precede non-return.

### Timeline and Ethical Notes

- Recruitment: 2–3 weeks (advertise via Age UK, libraries, community centres; incentivize with £15–20 gift vouchers).
- Interviews + observation: 4–5 weeks.
- Analysis: 2 weeks.
- **Informed consent**: All participants must understand data will be anonymised, used only for programme design, not individual assessment. Extra care with older adult participants: clear language, time to ask questions, written summary of findings sent back.

# Product Plan: Community Library Digital Skills Service

## Executive Summary

This service design project delivers five interrelated artefacts to operate a volunteer-led smartphone training programme for older adults (65+) in community libraries. The core challenge is not feature design but operational resilience: sustaining engagement through volunteer turnover, managing session efficacy under physical constraints, and designing confidence-building workflows that reduce emotional barriers to learning.

The plan prioritises delivery of a distinct, emotionally safe first session architecture; volunteer onboarding flows that reduce one-and-done attrition; and a coordinator toolkit that prevents single points of failure. Deliverables are grounded in evidence from Age UK's Digital Champion Programme, AbilityNet's one-to-one model, and international research on volunteer-integrated services. No digital product is built; all artefacts are operational and instructional.

---

## 1. What We Are Building (and What We Are Not)

### Deliverable 1: Service Blueprint

**Definition:** A systems diagram showing all actors (learner, tutor, coordinator, library), touchpoints (phone booking, SMS reminder, in-library signage, session materials), and data flows (attendance tracking, feedback loops, progression records).

**Why it matters:** The brief names volunteer turnover (39% annually) as a known risk. A service blueprint makes invisible failure modes visible: what happens when a tutor drops out mid-learner journey? Where does knowledge live? Who contacts the learner? A blueprint forces explicit answers before the service runs.

**Minimum viable version:** One diagram covering: learner entry → session one → session two+ → progression or dropout. Actors in swim lanes. Touchpoints marked. One failure path (tutor churn) explicitly resolved.

**What to cut:** Do not include aspirational elements like automated SMS or learner app tracking. Focus on what the coordinator can actually operate: phone calls, printed handouts, volunteer scheduling spreadsheets. No system integrations. No GDPR consent flows at this stage (see Open Decisions, below).

---

### Deliverable 2: User Journey Map

**Definition:** Narrative journey for the primary learner persona (high anxiety, concrete goal like "video call with grandchild" or "NHS appointment booking") through discovery, enrolment, first session, and three follow-up sessions.

**Why it matters:** Research shows dropout peaks between session one and two. A journey map identifies the emotional inflection points: the moment a learner feels unheard (when they're told "we'll start with email"), the moment they succeed ("I texted my daughter"), the moment a tutor absence threatens confidence. Mapping these moments guides the session structure design (Deliverable 4) and the volunteer training (Deliverable 3).

**Minimum viable version:** Four moments across four sessions. For each: what the learner wants (concrete), what anxieties are present (implicit), what the tutor must do (explicit), what success feels like (observable).

**What to cut:** Do not create separate journey maps for secondary personas (volunteer tutors, coordinator). One primary user journey is load-bearing; secondary personas get workflows instead (Deliverables 3 and 5). Do not include aspirational "ideal path" scenarios; focus on the likeliest path and the most common failure mode (session-one cognitive overload).

---

### Deliverable 3: Volunteer Onboarding Flow

**Definition:** A documented workflow covering tutor recruitment, matching with learners, training, session preparation, session execution, feedback, and retention touchpoints.

**Why it matters:** Research shows volunteer attrition is 39% and "one-and-done" mentality is a key driver. The Digital Champion Programme (Age UK) found that training + continuous support significantly reduced churn. A scripted onboarding flow ensures every tutor has the same entry experience, knows what to expect, has clear success criteria for session one, and receives structured touchpoints (check-in after session one, feedback before session two) that sustain engagement.

**Minimum viable version:** Five sections: (1) recruitment message (phone/email/volunteer fair), (2) intake questionnaire (what skills do you have? are you patient?), (3) one-session training (learner psychology, session structure, common problems, emergency escalation), (4) session prep checklist (device type, learner goal, materials), (5) post-session debrief (quick feedback form, recognition, next scheduling).

**What to cut:** Do not build a tutor vetting process. Do not create a test or certification scheme. Do not require background checks (unless coordinator legal advice demands it; not in scope here). Do not create a competency framework. Accept that tutors will be highly variable. The design must work with volunteers as they are, not train them to a standard they'll never meet.

---

### Deliverable 4: Session Structure (First Session and Follow-Up Template)

**Definition:** A scripted, time-boxed session design for Session One (approx. 3 hours, goal-focused, confidence-centric) and a repeatable template for Sessions Two onwards (1.5–2 hours, skill-progressive, problem-focused).

**Why it matters:** Research from effective programmes shows first sessions are structurally distinct: longer, slower, emotionally safe, concrete goal-driven, and designed to deliver a small, visible win. This is not the same as follow-on sessions. The brief identified this as a core design principle: "session one is a distinct artefact." Session One failure (tutor talks too fast, learner feels patronised, goal is unclear) directly predicts dropout before Session Two.

**Minimum viable version:** Session One: (1) welcome and goal clarification (15 min), (2) device setup and safety (45 min, hands-on), (3) first task (video call or SMS, learner-driven, tutor mirrors), (4) reflection and planning (15 min). Session Two+: (1) recap of last session and review of home practice (20 min), (2) one new task (tutor-led demo, then learner-led practice), (3) independent practice (learner alone, tutor available), (4) plan next steps (5 min).

**What to cut:** Do not design for classroom delivery to multiple learners. Sessions are one-to-one (or tutor + coordinator facilitating a pair). Do not create progression curricula (email → browsing → apps → messaging → payments). The learner's goal drives the curriculum, not a pre-set sequence. Do not include accessibility adaptations for each session (see Constraint and Deliverable 5). Do not mandate specific devices or operating systems; the session template must work for iPhone, Android, low-literacy, shaking hands, hearing loss—see Open Decisions, below.

---

### Deliverable 5: Key Touchpoints and Coordinator Toolkit

**Definition:** Documented, repeatable protocols for five critical interaction points outside scheduled sessions: pre-session booking confirmation, session reminder, post-session follow-up, learner absence handling, and tutor feedback loops.

**Why it matters:** Research emphasises that booking and reminder touchpoints (phone, SMS, printed invitation) significantly affect attendance. The coordinator is often a single point of failure (brief names this as a constraint). A toolkit of simple, low-tech protocols—phone call scripts, printed postcard templates, SMS reminder wording, absence follow-up decision tree—distributes this load and ensures consistency even if the coordinator changes.

**Minimum viable version:** Five one-page protocols: (1) booking confirmation (phone call, what to say, write the goal and date on a printed postcard, send same day); (2) reminder (phone call or SMS, 48 hours before, confirm the learner still wants to attend); (3) post-session note (thank you card, mention the win, preview next session); (4) absence (phone call within 48 hours, non-judgmental, ask what got in the way, reschedule); (5) tutor feedback (5-question form, tutor completes in 5 minutes, feeds into retention decision).

**What to cut:** Do not create a CRM or database system. Do not automate these touchpoints. Do not mandate specific tools (Whatsapp, SMS, phone). Accept that the coordinator may track learner data in a spreadsheet or on paper. Design for low-literacy coordinator input and high legibility output. Do not require data entry for every learner interaction. Do not build a learner app or tutor app; all communication is human-to-human with printed or spoken backup.

## 3. The Must-Nail Moment

### The Single Highest-Leverage Design Decision: Session One is Emotionally Safe, Goal-Driven, and Structurally Distinct

**What it is:** The design of the first session—its opening words, pacing, device handling, tutor posture, success definition, and closing ritual—is the single highest-leverage design decision in this service. Everything downstream depends on it.

**Why this one:** Research identifies dropout peaking between Session One and Two. The brief highlights "lower emotional stakes of not knowing" as a core problem: older adults learning digital skills are often anxious, sometimes ashamed, sometimes angry at technology. Session One is the moment when a tutor either builds emotional safety (learner feels heard, not rushed, not stupid) or erodes it (tutor assumes knowledge, goes too fast, pivots to tutor's agenda, uses jargon). Emotional safety in Session One predicts return. Failure at Session One is operationally expensive: lost learner, time sunk, volunteer confidence shaken, coordinator has to re-recruit.

**What failure looks like:**
- Tutor begins with "Let's start with the basics" (learner feels patronised, disengages).
- Tutor demonstrates email, learner watches passively (learner doesn't hold the device, doesn't move).
- Learner hasn't texted anyone by end of session (session happened to the learner, not for the learner; learner has no proof of progress).
- Learner leaves with no written confirmation of next session or tutor contact (learner feels abandoned; absence of next session is indistinguishable from service withdrawal).

The session structure (Deliverable 4) must be designed around this moment. The tutor training (Deliverable 3) must rehearse this moment. The journey map (Deliverable 2) must highlight the emotional vulnerabilities this moment must address. The service blueprint (Deliverable 1) must show what happens if Session One fails and how the coordinator recovers.

## 4. Hard Cuts

### Cut 1: No Learner Assessment or Progress Tracking System

The brief mentions "wide physical/cognitive variation" and "no single point of failure," yet a tempting design error is to create a progress tracker: levels (Bronze, Silver, Gold), pre-tests, post-tests, skill checklists. This is wrong for two reasons.

**First, operationally:** A progress tracking system requires the coordinator (already a single point of failure) or each tutor to maintain accurate data. Learners with low confidence may feel surveilled or judged by tests. Inconsistent data entry (one tutor logs videos watched, another doesn't) creates garbage that misleads the coordinator. Tracking becomes busywork that displaces the actual work: building confidence and drilling concrete skills.

**Second, psychologically:** Older adults learning digital skills often have prior negative school experiences. Assessment language (passing, failing, levels) retriggered that anxiety. The service is designed to build confidence, not assign grades. Progress is visible and felt by the learner (you video-called your daughter), not captured in a system. Trust that if Session One worked, the learner will show up to Session Two.

**What we do instead:** Tutors complete a 5-question post-session form (part of Deliverable 5): "Did the learner achieve their goal?" (yes/no), "How confident did they seem at the end?" (observed, not tested), "What will we do next session?" (next step, not level). The coordinator uses these forms to spot learners who missed their goal (check-in call) and tutors who are struggling (offer peer matching or end the pairing). No scoring, no levels, no learner-facing assessment.

---

### Cut 2: No App or Digital Tracking Platform

The brief permits "zero/low budget," and research shows that Age UK's successful Digital Champion Programme operated with phone calls and paper records. Resist the impulse to build a learner app (booking, progress, message the tutor), a tutor app (confirm availability, log hours), or a coordinator dashboard (see all learners in one place).

**Why this cut matters:** An app requires device compatibility support (iOS vs Android, OS versions, permissions), creates a second technical barrier for anxious learners (they're already struggling with smartphones; asking them to install an app adds friction), requires ongoing maintenance and security updates, and makes the service dependent on a third party (app store changes, payment processing, server uptime). A missing app update can silently break the service for learners mid-programme.

**What we do instead:** The coordinator maintains a simple spreadsheet or paper ledger: learner name, phone number, goal, tutor assigned, session dates, notes. The tutor texts or calls the learner 48 hours before a session. The learner confirms by phone call to the coordinator or tutor. No app. No data silo. Everything is human-readable and human-operable.

---

### Cut 3: No Formal Volunteer Competency Framework or Certification

The brief states "variable skill, no teaching training" as a given for volunteers. A plausible design error is to create a tutor certification: complete a 2-day training, pass a teaching skills assessment, get a badge. This over-formalises the role and excludes the volunteers most likely to succeed.

**Why this cut matters:** Older adult volunteers (who, research shows, have lower dropout rates than younger cohorts) often resist formal training. Framing tutoring as a taught skill requiring certification creates barrier to entry. Moreover, the best tutors for older learners are not professional educators; they're patient, tech-competent people who remember what it felt like to not understand something. A 2-day formal training selected for teaching technique, not empathy.

**What we do instead:** One 2–3 hour in-person training session covering: (1) how older learners think (psychology, not pedagogy), (2) the session structure they'll use (walkthrough), (3) the learner's goal (why it matters, how to keep focus on it), (4) common pitfalls and how to recover, (5) who to call if things go wrong (coordinator escalation). No test. No grading. Simple takeaway materials (one-page tutor checklists for each session type). Tutors learn by doing.

---

### Cut 4: No Device-Specific Curriculum or Hardware Loan Programme

The brief mentions "device heterogeneity (iOS + Android)" as a constraint, and research shows AbilityNet's successful model offers one-to-one training that adapts to the learner's device. A tempting design is to loan devices (library has 20 iPads, learners borrow them for sessions and home practice) or create separate curricula (iOS pathway, Android pathway, web pathway).

**Why this cut matters:** Device lending creates inventory management (tracking loans, device resets, damage), liability (learner damages device), and false equivalence (a loaned iPad is not the learner's phone; muscle memory and confidence don't transfer). Separate curricula doubles design and tutor training work, and forces learners into a device bin (you're Android; go with Android tutor). The principle is: tutors work with learners' own devices, no exceptions.

**What we do instead:** Tutors are trained to work with any device the learner brings. Session One includes 15 minutes of "device orientation" (where are settings? how do you unlock this? what's that button?). The session structure is device-agnostic: "We're going to send a text message" works identically on iPhone and Android; the buttons are in different places, but the task is the same. Tutors carry printed visual guides (iOS screenshots, Android screenshots, side by side) for common tasks.

---

### Cut 5: No Accessibility Adaptation per Learner or Session-Specific Accommodations Workflow

The brief identifies "accessibility floor" as a constraint and notes "wide physical/cognitive variation." A design temptation is to create an accessibility intake (hearing loss? poor eyesight? tremor?) and adaptive materials (large-print handouts, audio instructions, simplified language variants). While well-intentioned, this creates coordinator burden and stigmatises learners (filling in an accessibility form feels like special education).

**Why this cut matters:** Individual accommodation requests create a matrix of versions (large-print + audio, large-print + simplified, etc.) that the coordinator tracks and tutors prepare. One missing variant creates frustration. Tutors don't know which accommodation their learner needs until session day. Better to build universally legible materials that work for everyone: large text by default (20pt minimum, high contrast), short sentences, plain language, printed materials plus verbal walkthrough, hands-on practice (not lecture), clear buttons and short labels on screen, no jargon.

**What we do instead:** All materials (printed handouts, session plans, tutor checklists) are designed for high legibility by default: large type, high contrast, simple layout. Session One includes tutor asking "What helps you learn best? Do you prefer I show you, or we do it together? Do you like to take notes?" The tutor adapts in the moment, not from a pre-filled form. Accessibility is built in, not bolted on.

---

## 5. Open Decisions for the Design Director (Kenji Aldaine)

### Decision 1: Learner Data Storage and GDPR Compliance Model

**The question:** How does the coordinator store learner data (name, phone, goal, session notes) while meeting UK GDPR obligations? Specifically: where is the data stored (paper, spreadsheet, cloud service)? Who has access? When is it deleted? What happens if a learner drops out?

**Option A: Paper ledger + coordinator's laptop (no cloud)**
- **Upside:** No third-party data controller. Data stays physical (library). Coordinator has full control. No subscription costs. Simple.
- **Downside:** If coordinator leaves, data leaves (single point of failure). No backup if fire. Hard to report on numbers of learners served (useful for funding). Requires explicit GDPR documentation for anyone handling the paper (includes volunteers).
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises data minimalism, minimises tech burden, but creates organisational risk.

**Option B: Shared spreadsheet (Excel, Google Sheets, OneDrive) with explicit access control**
- **Upside:** Data is backed up automatically. Multiple people can access it (reduces single point of failure if coordinator sick). Easy to count learners and measure programme reach. Searchable.
- **Downside:** Cloud service (Microsoft, Google) is a third-party data processor; library must have a Data Processing Agreement (DPA) in place. Requires training for all users (tutors, coordinator) on not sharing login credentials. Requires a deletion protocol: when learner data is removed, old versions are still in cloud backup for 30 days.
- **Tradeoff:** Adds operational resilience (backup, multi-user access) but introduces compliance overhead (DPA, training, deletion audits).

**Option C: Standalone database or CRM tool (Airtable, Zoho, Pipedrive)**
- **Upside:** Purpose-built for data management. Role-based access (tutor sees only their learner; coordinator sees all). Built-in reporting. Can generate reports for funding applications.
- **Downside:** Subscription cost (library budget constraint). Requires vendor security audit (does vendor meet library's data protection standards?). Tutor and coordinator training required. If vendor goes down or charges prices up, library is locked in.
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises features and audit trails, but creates cost and vendor dependency.

**Recommendation for Kenji:** Start with Option B (shared spreadsheet + DPA). It's pragmatic: costs nothing, works today, and data remains in the UK (library's OneDrive or Google Workspace account). Require the library to sign a one-page DPA with the cloud provider (template exists; it's boilerplate). Train all users on the deletion protocol (data deleted after learner completes or drops out; deleted rows are purged when the coordinator does a quarterly clean). If the programme grows (100+ learners), revisit Option C. Do not start with Option C.

---

### Decision 2: Volunteer Matching Model (Trained Pool vs. Ad Hoc Tutor-Learner Pairing)

**The question:** Does the library maintain a roster of trained tutors, match learners to tutors, and sustain the pairing across multiple sessions? Or does each learner get whoever volunteers show up on the day they book?

**Option A: Trained roster with fixed pairings**
- **Upside:** Learner sees the same tutor across sessions (builds relationship, trust, continuity). Tutor knows the learner's goal progression (session one to three is a journey). Easier to onboard tutors (train once, use repeatedly). Tutor feels accountable.
- **Downside:** Volunteer absence (tutor gets sick, takes holiday) forces coordinator to find a substitute or reschedule learner. Building a roster requires identifying, training, and retaining 4–8 tutors per library (high recruitment bar). One tutor dropout can strand learners mid-journey.
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises continuity and relationship, but requires higher volunteer commitment and recruitment effort.

**Option B: Ad hoc pool; match on first available tutor**
- **Upside:** Flexible. Learner books a session; whoever volunteers that day is available. Easy to scale: add more tutors to the pool as demand increases. No learner stranded if one tutor quits. Low tutor commitment barrier (show up when you can).
- **Downside:** Learner meets different tutor each session (no continuity; has to re-explain goal). Tutor doesn't know learner's progress (session two tutor doesn't know what session one covered). Knowledge lives in learner's head, not in handover notes. Learner feels less invested if tutor changes. Harder to train: tutors trained once, never reinforced, one-and-done attrition likely.
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises tutor flexibility, minimises retention risk, but sacrifices continuity and learner experience.

**Option C: Hybrid—fixed primary tutor + trained backup**
- **Upside:** Learner has one assigned tutor for continuity. If assigned tutor unavailable, a trained backup covers (learner sees same backup twice, consistency). Reduces single tutor dependency. Builds a small roster (2–3 tutors per learner cohort) without scaling to 8.
- **Downside:** Requires explicit handover protocol between primary and backup (session notes, learner's goal, home practice feedback). More admin for coordinator to track. Backup tutor must be trained to the same standard as primary.
- **Tradeoff:** Balanced approach; requires clear documentation (Deliverable 5) and discipline.

**Recommendation for Kenji:** Implement Option C (fixed primary + trained backup). It's robust: learner gets continuity but service survives tutor absence. Handover is simple (one-page session note that backup reads before arriving). The backup arrangement also gives tutors a peer relationship (primary tutor and backup coordinate, reducing isolation). Document the handover protocol in Deliverable 5 as a specific touchpoint.

---

### Decision 3: Session Venue and Noise/Privacy Management

**The question:** All sessions happen in the library's shared space (noisy, public, interruptions) or is a quiet room reserved? What if no quiet room is available?

**Option A: Dedicated quiet room for every session**
- **Upside:** No background noise (learner hears instructions clearly). Privacy (older adults sometimes feel embarrassed learning tech; closed door reduces peer observation). Tutor can use speaker or earbuds for video call demo without disturbing others. Consistent environment across learners.
- **Downside:** Requires library to reserve a meeting room per session (may not be available, especially busy libraries). Learner may feel isolated or under surveillance in a closed room. Coordinator must manage a room booking system (another point of failure). If room not available, session is postponed (disrupts learner momentum).
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises privacy and acoustics, but creates scheduling complexity and learner isolation risk.

**Option B: Open library space, but structured session "pod" (designated table, printed signage)**
- **Upside:** No room reservation needed. Session is visible (library staff and other users can see there's a learning event; normalises digital learning). Learner is in the public space where they'd normally use the library (familiarity). Low coordination overhead.
- **Downside:** Background noise (group study, children's area, printing). If learner is on a video call, others hear the caller. Other users may interrupt (ask questions, sit down). Tutor and learner may feel exposed. Hard to focus on details (small text, quiet instructions).
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises operational simplicity, but sacrifices learner focus and emotional safety.

**Option C: Hybrid—Book a quiet room if available; otherwise, use a designated library area with noise-dampening protocol**
- **Upside:** Preferred: quiet room. Fallback: in-library space with tutor and learner agreeing on a non-disruptive area (corner table, away from printers), tutor speaking clearly, learner holding device at eye level so others don't see the screen. Flexibility.
- **Downside:** Requires tutor and learner to collaborate on managing a less-than-ideal space. Requires a fallback plan (what do tutors do to focus a learner if there's noise?).
- **Tradeoff:** Balanced; acknowledges library reality (no dedicated rooms) while still optimising when possible.

**Recommendation for Kenji:** Implement Option C (book quiet room if available; fallback to designated area with protocol). Document the fallback protocol in Deliverable 5: tutor talks at normal volume (not loud, not whispered; clear), learner chooses whether to use speaker for video calls or earbuds, session materials are printed (not on-screen only, to reduce glare and light-reading difficulty), first 5 minutes of session is a focus ritual (tutor and learner agree to put phones away except the learning device, check time for next appointment). The fallback is not ideal, but it's explicit and prepared for, not improvised.

---

### Decision 4: Tutor Recruitment and "One-and-Done" Attrition Prevention

**The question:** How does the library identify and recruit volunteer tutors? And what distinguishes a tutor who returns for multiple sessions vs. one who tutors once and disappears (the 39% attrition risk)?

**Option A: Recruit from existing library volunteers + local tech companies (corporate volunteer days)**
- **Upside:** Library volunteers are already trained in library systems and values. Tech company employees have strong device skills. Both groups may have time (retirees volunteer regularly; companies allocate paid volunteer time). Low barrier to get people in the door.
- **Downside:** Existing library volunteers may not be tech-confident. Tech company volunteers are transactional (one day, corporate requirement, then gone). Neither group has chosen to commit to ongoing tutor role. High one-and-done risk.
- **Tradeoff:** Easy recruitment, but weak retention.

**Option B: Recruit specifically for long-term commitment; emphasise "ongoing role" in messaging**
- **Upside:** Self-selects for tutors who want to tutor (not just comply with employer requirement). Messaging is explicit: "We're looking for tutors who commit to 6+ learners over 6 months." Attracts people drawn to sustained mentoring.
- **Downside:** Smaller recruitment pool (not everyone wants to commit). Takes longer to build roster. Requires explicit commitment conversation (increases friction for casual interest).
- **Tradeoff:** Smaller pool, but higher retention likelihood and stronger tutor-learner relationships.

**Option C: Recruit flexibly; build retention through structured touchpoints (post-session feedback, peer community, recognition)**
- **Upside:** Low entry barrier (show up when you can). Retention is designed in, not recruited in. Even tutors with light availability (one session per month) are valuable. Post-session feedback and recognition rituals (thank-you card, peer celebration) reward tutor contribution. Peer community (tutors know each other, share problems) reduces isolation and one-and-done impulse.
- **Downside:** Requires deliberate retention design (Deliverable 5 must include tutor feedback and recognition touchpoints). Doesn't guarantee ongoing commitment; tutors may still fade. Coordinator must actively reach out to tutors between sessions (more coordinator load).
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises accessibility and volunteer autonomy, but requires ongoing relationship tending.

**Recommendation for Kenji:** Implement Option C (flexible recruitment + structured retention). The research is clear: 39% attrition is not solved by recruitment messaging; it's solved by making tutors feel part of a supported community. Design into Deliverable 5 a tutor feedback loop (post-session form that tutor completes, coordinator reads it and sends a thank-you note or a "we matched you with another learner!" message within 48 hours). A tutor who gets positive feedback after Session One is far more likely to return than one who tutors once and hears nothing. Create a tutor email list or WhatsApp group so tutors can ask peers for advice ("How do I explain WiFi?"). Celebrate wins: "This week, 5 learners sent their first text message; thanks to you." Small recognition, sustained contact, peer community—these prevent one-and-done.

---

### Decision 5: Session Location Accessibility (Accessibility of the Library Building and Transport)

**The question:** How do we ensure learners with mobility issues, visual impairments, or hearing loss can physically attend sessions? Is this a library problem or a programme design problem?

**Option A: Design assumes the library is accessible (lever, accessible parking, disabled toilet, lift); out-of-scope for this project**
- **Upside:** Focuses design effort on the service (sessions, tutoring) not the building. Library accessibility is already mandated by building regulations (Equality Act 2010).
- **Downside:** If the library is not accessible, learners are excluded before they even arrive. Tutor cannot overcome a flight of stairs. No recourse in the service design.
- **Tradeoff:** Shifts responsibility to library management; service design assumes the foundation is sound.

**Option B: Conduct an accessibility audit of the library (parking, entry, corridors, toilets, room access) and include findings and recommendations in Deliverable 1 (Service Blueprint)**
- **Upside:** Surface the issue explicitly. If library is not accessible, it's documented. Library can decide whether to improve the building or offer alternative arrangements (home visits, video sessions). Service design is informed by building reality, not assumption.
- **Downside:** Adds scope (building audit is outside service design). May uncover expensive findings (no accessible parking; learners with mobility issues cannot attend). Creates tension between service ambition and building constraint.
- **Tradeoff:** Honest assessment of barriers; increases scope and potential conflict.

**Option C: Design transport and in-home backup options into the service**
- **Upside:** Maximises reach. If learner cannot get to library (mobility, transport anxiety, no driver's license), offer home visits (tutor visits learner) or video sessions (tutor and learner on video call, learner at home, tutor in library). Learner choice: attend in-person or remote.
- **Downside:** Home visits require tutor vetting (safeguarding, DBS check) and additional training (working in a home vs. library). Video sessions require learner to have internet access, a camera-enabled device, and enough confidence to use video (not always present for anxious learners). Coordinator must match learner to delivery mode (another decision point).
- **Tradeoff:** Maximises inclusion, but adds complexity and safeguarding overhead.

**Recommendation for Kenji:** Implement Option B + C hybrid. Include in Deliverable 1 (Service Blueprint) a brief accessibility audit: check the library's disability access (parking, entry, accessible toilet, quiet space availability). If accessible, assume learners arrive in-person. If not accessible (common in older UK libraries), document this. Then design transport support into Deliverable 5: provide tutor with public transport links and cost (learner can ask library or tutor for help getting there), and offer an alternative delivery mode for learners who cannot travel (home visit by tutor, or video session if learner has internet). Require home-visit tutors to do a 30-minute safeguarding briefing (how to take notes without making learner feel surveilled, what boundaries to maintain). This balances service ambition with practical constraint.

## 6. Success Signals

Success signals are observable behaviours that tell us the service is working. They are not targets (invented numbers), but behavioural evidence.

### Signal 1: Learner Returns After Session One

**Observable behaviour:** A learner who completes Session One shows up for Session Two (or calls the coordinator to reschedule, not disappears).

**Why this matters:** Dropout between Session One and Two is the single biggest risk the service faces. If Session One fails emotionally (tutor talks at learner, learner feels stupid, or learner's goal is unclear), learner doesn't return—and this service has failed its core function. If learner returns to Session Two without prompting (no desperate coordinator calls), it means Session One worked: emotional safety was real, goal was clear, learner had a small win (sent a text, made a call). This signals service efficacy at the hardest moment.

**How we'll know:** Attendance tracking (Deliverable 5 coordinator toolkit includes a simple log: Session One attendance, Session Two attendance, did learner show up or cancel?). Target: no invented number. Signal: if 60%+ of Session One learners also attend Session Two, service design is working. If fewer than 40%, Session One design has failed; design must be reworked.

---

### Signal 2: Tutor Completes at Least Two Sessions with the Same Learner

**Observable behaviour:** A tutor is matched with a learner for Session One, and the tutor shows up again for Session Two with the same learner (or explicitly asks to continue with a different learner, indicating they want to tutor at all).

**Why this matters:** Research identifies "one-and-done" tutors (39% annual attrition) as a key failure mode. If a tutor attends one session and never returns or never volunteers again, the service is consuming tutors without sustaining them. If a tutor completes two sessions with the same learner, it signals: (1) the tutor felt capable and supported (not abandoned after Session One), (2) the learner benefited enough that tutor wants to continue the relationship, or (3) tutor understands continuity matters (same learner is better than different learner each week). Any of these is a signal that tutor retention design (onboarding, feedback, recognition, peer community) is working.

**How we'll know:** Tutor attendance log (part of Deliverable 5 toolkit) tracks tutor name, learner name, session number. A tutor who appears twice with the same learner (or explicitly opts into a different learner) signals retention. No invented target. Signal: if fewer than 50% of tutors complete two sessions with any learner, tutor retention design has failed; onboarding or support touchpoints need rework.

---

### Signal 3: Learner Achieves Their Stated Goal (Concrete, Observable)

**Observable behaviour:** A learner states a goal in Session One (e.g., "I want to video call my granddaughter," "I want to book a GP appointment online," "I want to text my son"), and by Session Three or Four, the tutor observes the learner doing this goal independently (learner demonstrates the task, or learner reports they have done it at home).

**Why this matters:** The service exists to serve concrete learner goals, not to teach "digital skills" in the abstract. If a learner shows up but never achieves their goal, the service has failed—attendance is just activity, not outcome. If a learner independently executes their stated goal (video calls daughter without tutor present, or books an appointment without calling the tutor for help), it signals: (1) service transferred knowledge and confidence from tutor to learner, (2) learner is no longer anxious about this task (confidence is visible in independent action), (3) learner will likely use this skill at home (behaviour change is durable).

**How we'll know:** Post-session tutor feedback form (Deliverable 5) includes a simple question: "Did the learner achieve their goal today or make progress toward it?" (yes/no). By Session Three, coordinator reviews feedback: if learner has achieved goal, note it (learner has a win; tutor knows it). If learner has not achieved goal by Session Three, coordinator calls learner (check-in: what's in the way?) and tutor (extra support?). No invented target. Signal: if 70%+ of learners achieve their stated goal within four sessions, service design is working. If fewer than 50% achieve goals, either goal-setting in Session One is unclear, or session structure is not supporting transfer to independent practice.

---

## Implementation Notes

This plan assumes the following:

1. **Library has a coordinator** (paid staff, single person or job-shared) who owns the service, books sessions, recruits tutors, maintains records, and escalates problems. The service design assumes this person exists and is supported; it does not create a system that works without one.

2. **Library has a quiet or semi-quiet space** available, booked per session. If not available, the fallback protocol (Deliverable 5, Decision 3) applies.

3. **Library does not have existing digital skills programming** that this service must integrate with. If it does, service design should align with existing library policies (GDPR, volunteer vetting, booking systems).

4. **Volunteer tutors are recruited locally** (via word of mouth, volunteer centres, employer networks, community boards). The service design does not create a recruitment campaign; coordinator handles recruitment as part of their role, supported by Deliverable 3 (onboarding flow) to make retention easier.

5. **Learners contact the library by phone or in-person** to book sessions. No online booking, no app, no website form (acknowledges that many older learners lack confidence with online booking).

6. **All documentation is printed** (not PDF-only) and available in large type, high contrast, plain language. Digital versions are available but not required.

7. **Success is measured by observation and simple tracking**, not complex data analysis. Coordinator logs attendance, feedback, and goal achievement on a spreadsheet; no analytics platform needed.

## What Happens After Delivery

This plan specifies design outputs (blueprints, journeys, scripts, protocols), not implementation. After delivery:

1. **Library coordinator trains** with the onboarding flow and session structure. Coordinator rehearses recruiting tutors, running Session One, handling an absence. Issues and fixes are fed back to design (one revision round expected).

2. **First cohort of tutors and learners** pilot the service. Expected: 4–8 tutors, 8–12 learners over 12 weeks.

3. **Coordinator logs attendance, feedback, and learner goal achievement** (Deliverable 5 toolkit). By week 12, review success signals (Learner return rate, tutor repeat rate, goal achievement). Expected: one or more signals will highlight a design gap (e.g., Session One is too long, or tutor feedback form is too tedious to complete).

4. **Minor iteration on the design** based on pilot feedback. Revise the session structure, onboarding, or protocols if needed. Do not redesign from scratch.

5. **Scale or sustain:** If pilot is successful, expand to a second cohort with the same design. Coordinator stays in role and recruits more tutors. No new design is needed unless the library context changes (e.g., library closure, new coordinator, shift to remote delivery).

---

## Conclusion

This product plan is built around one core insight: **the service's success or failure is determined by Session One and tutor retention, not by feature scope or technology**. Every deliverable is designed to support these two moments. The service blueprint makes failure modes visible so they can be designed out. The user journey identifies the emotional vulnerabilities Session One must address. The tutor onboarding ensures every tutor has the knowledge and confidence to deliver Session One safely. The session structure is the detailed script for that moment. The coordinator toolkit ensures that the service survives tutor absence, learner anxiety, and forgetfulness. Nothing is aspirational; everything is operationally achievable by a coordinator, four to eight tutors, and a quiet corner of a library.

Success is not measured by user engagement metrics, app retention, or learning level completions. Success is measured by observable behaviour: learners return after Session One, tutors return after Session One, learners independently achieve their goals. These three signals tell us the service is working.

# Community Library Smartphone Tutoring Programme
## Service Design Document
**For older adults (65+) learning smartphone and digital skills**

---

## 1. Service Blueprint: Swim-Lane Narrative

### Stage A: Discovery and Enquiry

#### Learner (Front-stage)
The older adult hears about the programme through a friend, library poster, or local radio. They visit the library or call the main desk asking "Do you teach mobile phones?" They may express doubt ("I've never used one of these things") or curiosity ("My grandchild says I should learn"). They want simple, no-pressure information: who teaches, what it costs, how many sessions, and whether they have to come in person.

#### Tutor (Back-stage)
Tutors are not yet involved. The tutor pool exists but is dormant at this stage.

#### Coordinator (Back-stage)
The library coordinator (or dedicated programme coordinator) answers the enquiry by phone or in person. They listen without judgment to the older adult's concerns, confirm the programme is free, and ask one screening question: "What would you like to do on your phone that you can't do now?" This reveals the goal. They then explain: "We'll match you with a volunteer tutor for four one-hour sessions over four weeks. Your first session is three hours so we get to know each other and understand what you want to learn."

The coordinator logs the enquiry in a simple spreadsheet (name, phone, email if available, goal, best time to call). They confirm next steps: "I'll call you back within two working days with dates and your tutor's name."

#### Library (Systems)
The library information desk has a one-page flyer explaining the programme (plain language, no jargon). The flyer has one phone number and one email address to contact. Posters are placed in the community notice board and local GP surgeries with the same contact details. Local radio has run a thirty-second advert naming the library and repeating the same phone number.

**Materials:**
- One-page flyer (A5, large print 18pt, single fold)
- Poster (A3, 24pt heading)
- Phone script for coordinator intake

**Where failure can occur:**
- Older adult does not remember the phone number or how to call
- Older adult feels the information is too technical or uses words they don't understand
- Older adult does not get a callback within the promised timeframe
- Coordinator makes assumptions about the learner's goal rather than listening

**How failure is recovered:**
- Flyer includes QR code linking to a simple text version on the library website (large text, high contrast)
- Coordinator keeps a checklist: all enquiries logged same day, callback within two working days
- If no callback within 48 hours, learner can call back and ask for the coordinator by name
- Coordinator is trained to reflect back what the learner said: "So you want to video call your daughter in Australia — is that right?"

---

### Stage B: Booking

#### Learner (Front-stage)
The coordinator calls the older adult back with three dates for the first three-hour session and one name: their tutor's name (e.g., "Your tutor's name is Michael, and he's been volunteering with us for two years"). The learner chooses a date and time that suits them. If they don't have a phone they can answer or are unsure, the coordinator says, "That's fine — we'll send you a letter with all the details in big print."

The learner might feel nervous at this point ("What if I'm too slow?"). The coordinator says: "Michael has worked with lots of people learning from scratch. There's no pressure and no test."

#### Tutor (Back-stage)
The tutor is notified by the coordinator via email or phone call: "Your learner is [Name], aged [age if shared], wants to [goal]. First session is [date and time] at [location]. It's three hours. Please call her/him by [date] to introduce yourself." The tutor has a phone script (see Volunteer Onboarding Flow section).

#### Coordinator (Back-stage)
The coordinator records the booking in the spreadsheet, notes the tutor name, and sends (or the tutor sends) a simple email or letter confirming:
- Date, time, location, and what door to use
- First name of the tutor and his/her phone number (so learner can contact them)
- A line drawing or photo of the library entrance so they can find it
- "Come 10 minutes early so we can settle in"

The coordinator also creates a simple checklist for the tutor: Day 1 tasks, materials to bring, session outline.

#### Library (Systems)
A room is reserved for the session (a quiet, bright room with a table, two comfortable chairs, a charger, and WiFi that works). The library makes sure the room is set aside on the calendar as "Smartphone Tutoring" so staff don't book it for something else.

**Materials:**
- Confirmation letter (A5, 16pt type, 1.5 line spacing)
- Tutor briefing email
- Session room checklist
- Session outline (one page, for tutor)

**Where failure can occur:**
- Older adult loses the booking confirmation and does not remember the time
- Tutor does not call to introduce himself, and learner feels anxious
- Room is double-booked or is too noisy for concentration
- Learner arrives but does not know which door to use

**How failure is recovered:**
- Coordinator sends reminder 48 hours before (see Stage C)
- Tutor introduction call is mandatory (scripted, takes five minutes, happens 3–5 days before session)
- Room is blocked on shared calendar with a note: "DO NOT BOOK"
- Photo of library entrance is in confirmation letter
- Coordinator or library staff are briefed to greet the learner by name when they arrive

---

### Stage C: Pre-Session (Confirmation and Reminder)

#### Learner (Front-stage)
Two days before the session, the learner receives a reminder call or SMS (learner choice, recorded at booking). The coordinator or a trained volunteer reads from a script: "Hi [name], I'm calling from the library to remind you that your first tutoring session is on [day] at [time]. Michael will be there. If you need to reschedule, please call me back on this number. We're looking forward to seeing you."

The learner might ask: "Do I need to bring anything?" The answer is always: "No, just bring yourself. Michael will have everything you need."

If the learner cannot make it, they call back and reschedule. The same process repeats.

#### Tutor (Back-stage)
The tutor receives a checklist from the coordinator the day before:
- Learner name, age (if provided), goal
- Any accessibility notes (hearing difficulty, mobility, vision)
- Session timing and location
- Materials to bring: notebook, phone (sample device if needed), charger, name badge
- Session outline with timings (see Session Structure section)
- Tutor script for opening (welcome, goal elicitation)

The tutor reviews the script and prepares any device-specific notes (e.g., if the goal is "text my daughter," the tutor notes which apps to show).

#### Coordinator (Back-stage)
The coordinator makes all reminder calls or sends SMS on day 2 before the session. They log each call (reached or not reached, any notes). If the learner cannot be reached, the coordinator emails or posts a reminder postcard (arrives day before or same day).

If the learner says they cannot come, the coordinator reschedules immediately and updates the tutor.

#### Library (Systems)
The room is prepared: chairs are clean, table is cleared, charger is tested, WiFi is tested, any tissue boxes or water bottles are out. A sign on the door says "Smartphone Tutoring Session — [name] — Do Not Disturb."

**Materials:**
- Reminder script (for coordinator or volunteer caller)
- Tutor pre-session checklist
- Device checklist (phone, charger, sample devices if needed)
- Room prep checklist
- Reminder postcard (if needed)

**Where failure can occur:**
- Learner does not answer phone and does not get reminder
- Learner forgets despite reminder
- Tutor does not arrive or calls in sick at last minute
- Room is not ready (WiFi down, no charger, too cold/hot)

**How failure is recovered:**
- Postcard is sent as backup to reminder call (arrives 1–2 days before)
- Learner is given written confirmation with large print date/time/location (Stage B)
- Coordinator has a backup tutor list and contacts one by phone immediately if primary tutor cancels
- Library staff are trained on room setup and check it together (checklist) 2 hours before session start
- Coordinator or library manager is on-call to troubleshoot WiFi or room issues

---

### Stage D: Session One (3 hours)

#### Learner (Front-stage)
The learner arrives 10 minutes early (or on time) and is welcomed by name. Tutor and learner settle in a quiet room with a phone and a cup of tea. The tutor opens by saying: "Hi [name], I'm Michael. I'm really glad you're here. Let me tell you how today works, and then I want to hear what you want to learn."

For the next 20 minutes, the tutor listens to the learner's goal and concerns. Then the learner holds a phone for the first time (or first time with this tutor) and the tutor explains each part: screen, button, how to turn it on, how to unlock it. The learner tries each action (with support). Then for 60 minutes, the learner works on their chosen task (e.g., making a phone call to a family member, or texting). The tutor sits beside them, narrates what they're doing, and lets them control the phone. There's a 15-minute break for tea and chat. Then for 45 minutes, the learner either continues that task or does a related one (e.g., saving a contact, taking a photo). Finally, for 15 minutes, tutor and learner talk about what worked, what to practice at home, and when they'll meet next.

The learner leaves with:
- A written reminder (handwritten by the tutor) of one thing to practice at home
- The tutor's phone number
- The next session date
- A sense of achievement (they've done something on the phone they've never done before)

#### Tutor (Back-stage)
The tutor arrives 30 minutes early to set up the phone, test it, and take three slow, deep breaths. During the session, the tutor follows the session outline (see Section 4). The tutor narrates everything: "I'm pressing the green button to make a call. Now I type your number. Now I press Call." The tutor lets the learner hold the phone and try, and does not grab it back or fix their mistakes for them — the learner learns by doing.

After the session, the tutor fills out a one-page feedback form (see Coordinator Protocols section) with: learner name, goal achieved (yes/no/partial), confidence level (1–5), what the learner will practice at home, anything to watch for in session 2, and any concerns. The tutor writes the practise reminder by hand in large, clear writing.

#### Coordinator (Back-stage)
The coordinator is not in the room but is available by phone if there's an emergency. After the session, they collect the tutor feedback form within 2 hours.

#### Library (Systems)
The library room is set up as described in Stage C. Staff members are briefed not to knock on the door or interrupt (sign on door). Refreshments are available (tea, coffee, water, biscuits) in the adjacent waiting area. A staff member checks in after 90 minutes to silently offer more tea.

**Materials:**
- Session One outline (three-hour script, with time markers)
- Tutor script for opening (goal elicitation)
- Tutor script for device orientation
- Tutor script for first task
- Tutor script for break and second task
- Tutor script for reflection and planning
- Handwritten reminder card (blank, for tutor to fill in)
- Tutor feedback form
- Sample smartphone(s) (if learner does not have own device)
- Charger

**Where failure can occur:**
- Tutor talks too fast or uses technical words (e.g., "swipe," "app," "WiFi password")
- Learner does not get to touch the phone enough
- Learner feels ashamed or stupid and does not speak up
- Tutor does not know what to do if phone runs out of battery or WiFi drops
- Learner does not understand the handwritten reminder card

**How failure is recovered:**
- Tutor training includes a "language guide" (see Design Tokens section) — avoid jargon, use "tap" not "click," use "the part at the top" not "status bar"
- Tutor is trained to hand the phone to the learner in the first 5 minutes and let them hold it for most of the session
- Tutor training includes normalising language: "Everyone finds this tricky at first. You're doing really well"
- Backup phone and charger are in the room; tutor knows how to switch to backup
- WiFi troubleshooting guide is taped to the room wall (simple steps, with phone number for library IT)
- Reminder card is written in large (18pt), dark print; tutor writes it slowly while talking about it; learner keeps it and can ask about it in Session 2

---

### Stage E: Sessions 2–4 (90 minutes each)

#### Learner (Front-stage)
Sessions 2, 3, and 4 follow the same time and location (consistency helps). The learner arrives, and the tutor asks: "How did the practice go? Did you text anyone? Did you take any photos?" The learner reports on what they did at home (or didn't do, which is fine). Then the tutor introduces something new that builds on session 1, or deepens the same skill. For example, if session 1 was "make a phone call," session 2 might be "save a contact" and "make a call to that contact." Session 3 might be "send a text message." Session 4 might be "send a photo."

Each skill is broken into tiny steps. The learner always gets to try. After 45 minutes of new learning, there's 20 minutes of independent practice (tutor sits back, learner tries without narration, tutor only helps if asked). Then the tutor and learner look ahead: "Next week, you could try... or we could focus more on..."

The learner leaves with another handwritten reminder card and feels increasingly confident.

#### Tutor (Back-stage)
The tutor follows the 90-minute session template (see Section 4). The tutor brings the previous reminder card and checks it off together: "Let me see what I wrote last week... did you practice this?" The tutor has notes from session 1 (the feedback form) and adjusts the pace if needed. If the learner seemed confused about something, the tutor returns to it. If they seemed bored, the tutor moves faster or introduces something harder.

At the end of session 4, the tutor asks: "What do you want to keep practicing?" and writes this on the final reminder card.

#### Coordinator (Back-stage)
The coordinator does not attend but receives tutor feedback forms after each session (within 2 hours). If there's a pattern (e.g., "learner confused about passwords three times," or "learner very confident now"), the coordinator flags it. After session 4, the coordinator reads all four feedback forms and prepares to contact the learner for goal achievement (Stage F).

#### Library (Systems)
The same room is used each week. Refreshments continue. A staff member learns the learner's name and greets them warmly.

**Materials:**
- Session 2–4 outline (90-minute script)
- Tutor script for recap
- Tutor script for new task
- Tutor script for independent practice
- Tutor script for next-steps planning
- Handwritten reminder cards (four total)
- Tutor feedback forms (one per session)

**Where failure can occur:**
- Learner does not complete home practice and feels they've failed
- Tutor forgets what happened in previous session
- Learner plateaus or loses motivation
- Learner wants to learn something completely different that's not in the original goal

**How failure is recovered:**
- Tutor opens each session: "Home practice is optional — the most important thing is that you came back. Tell me what you did"
- Tutor feedback form is reviewed before each session; tutor brings notes from previous week
- Coordinator monitors feedback forms and contacts learner (not tutor) to check in: "How's it going? Is this still what you want to learn?"
- Tutor is empowered to adjust the plan: "You're doing so well with texting — do you want to focus on that instead?"

---

### Stage F: Goal Achievement and Exit

#### Learner (Front-stage)
After session 4, the learner may feel ready to continue practicing alone, or may want more sessions. The coordinator calls the learner and says: "I'd like to celebrate what you've learned. Can I ask you: when you started, you wanted to [original goal]. Can you do that now?" The learner confirms (or learns something is still tricky). The coordinator says: "I'm proud of you. You've kept coming back and trying. Here's what you could practice on your own..." and offers a simple one-page guide with three ideas for practice (e.g., "Call three different people," "Text one person every day," "Take photos and share them").

The learner is invited to a small celebration event (tea and cake at the library, with other learners and tutors, once per month) and is added to an optional email or postcard bulletin that shares tips and invites them to drop-in practice sessions (free, optional, no tutor).

If the learner wants to continue, they can do so (coordinator arranges more sessions or the learner can attend drop-in practice).

#### Tutor (Back-stage)
The tutor is thanked formally by the coordinator: "Thank you for [name]'s progress. They're now confident with [skill]. Here's what they're going to practice next." The tutor is invited to the monthly celebration event and is given recognition (certificate, public thank-you, or feature in the library newsletter).

#### Coordinator (Back-stage)
The coordinator calls the learner (within one week of session 4) to confirm goal achievement. They record the outcome (goal fully achieved, partially achieved, not yet achieved, learner wants to continue). If goal not achieved, the coordinator offers more sessions or identifies barriers: "Was it hard to find the time? Was something unclear? Do you want to come back next month?"

The coordinator sends a thank-you postcard to the tutor. They flag the learner for optional follow-up contact: "How's your practice going?" (postcard sent four weeks after session 4, then once quarterly).

#### Library (Systems)
The library hosts the monthly celebration event (30–60 minutes, in the main library space, free tea and cake). Tutors, learners, and library staff attend. The library coordinator or manager gives a brief thank-you speech and mentions one or two learner achievements by name (always with permission). A photo is taken (if everyone consents) for the library newsletter.

The library includes a simple one-page practice guide in the learner's exit pack (large print, no jargon, three concrete ideas).

**Materials:**
- Goal achievement call script (coordinator)
- One-page practice guide (three ideas, 18pt type)
- Thank-you postcard (to tutor)
- Volunteer recognition certificate or newsletter feature
- Monthly celebration event invitation (letter and email)
- Post-exit follow-up postcard (four weeks later, optional check-in)

**Where failure can occur:**
- Learner does not want to admit the goal was not achieved (shame)
- Tutor never gets formally thanked and does not return
- Learner loses motivation after the programme ends
- Learner does not know what to practise on their own

**How failure is recovered:**
- Coordinator frames the call as "celebration," not assessment: "Let's talk about all the things you can do now"
- If goal not fully achieved, coordinator normalises it: "That's totally normal — some things take longer to learn. Do you want to keep going?" (offer is genuine)
- Tutor is thanked in writing within one week, invited to celebration event, and asked: "Would you like to tutor again?" (immediately)
- Learner is invited to optional monthly drop-in practice (no tutor, peer-led, social aspect keeps motivation)
- Practice guide is laminated, large print, kept simple (three things, not thirty)
- Learner is given tutor's phone number: "Feel free to call me if you get stuck"

## 2. User Journey Map: Older Adult Learner

### Moment 1: Discovery

**What the learner wants:**
- To understand that learning a smartphone is possible for someone "like me"
- To find the programme without having to ask their grandchild for help
- To know it will not cost money or involve a test
- To believe there are other older people doing this

**Anxieties present:**
- "Smartphones are for young people — I'll never get it"
- "I'll be the only person who doesn't know how to use one"
- "If I mess up, I'll break it"
- "I don't have a phone so I can't even start"
- "I'll be too slow"

**What the service does:**
- Advertises in places they already go: library, GP surgery, community notice board, local radio
- Uses plain language on flyers and posters (no jargon, large print, high contrast)
- Mentions other learners ("We've taught 40 people this year")
- Explains it's free and matches you with "a volunteer tutor" (friendly, not a teacher)
- Includes a simple phone number to call and a QR code for online info
- The person who answers is warm and listens without judgment

**Emotional outcome should be:**
- Relief ("This exists and it's for me")
- Hope ("I could actually learn this")
- Permission ("It's okay that I don't know — I'm not alone")

---

### Moment 2: Booking and Waiting

**What the learner wants:**
- To pick a day that suits their life (shopping day, doctor's day, grandchild visit)
- To know the tutor's name and something about them (are they patient? young? old like me?)
- To have the information written down so they won't forget
- To be called back as promised

**Anxieties present:**
- "What if I get the date wrong?"
- "What if they ask me questions I can't answer on the phone?"
- "What if the tutor is impatient with me?"
- "What if I can't find the library room?"

**What the service does:**
- Coordinator calls back within two days (keeps promise)
- Asks three simple questions (no interrogation): name, goal, best time
- Offers three dates for the first session (control)
- Gives the tutor's first name and a line: "He's been volunteering for two years and he's very patient"
- Sends a written confirmation with the date, time, tutor's name, and a photo of the library door
- If no phone, sends it by post
- Offers to send a reminder closer to the date

**Emotional outcome should be:**
- Confidence ("I've got this written down, I won't forget")
- Personal connection ("Michael knows my name, I know his")
- Safety ("They're keeping their promise")

---

### Moment 3: Session One

**What the learner wants:**
- To hold a real phone and try something (not just watch)
- To be told "that's right" or "let's try a different way" (not "you're doing it wrong")
- To learn one thing they can tell their family: "I made a call today"
- To feel like they spent time with someone who cared
- To have written instructions for what to practice at home

**Anxieties present:**
- "Everyone here knows more than me"
- "I'm going to break it or delete something I shouldn't"
- "This is all happening too fast"
- "I don't understand what words like 'app' mean"

**What the service does:**
- Tutor greets them by name and sits next to them (not across a desk)
- Tutor explains everything in plain language: "I'm going to press the green button. It's here" (points)
- Tutor puts the phone in the learner's hands in the first 5 minutes
- Tutor does the task once slowly while narrating, then steps back
- Tutor says "You're doing great" or "Let's try that again together" (never shame)
- Tutor writes a reminder card by hand (big letters) while talking through what to practice
- Tutor gives their phone number: "Call me if you get stuck"

**Emotional outcome should be:**
- Pride ("I did something I've never done before")
- Competence ("I could actually do that")
- Safety ("That person wasn't judging me")
- Ownership ("I've got instructions I wrote with her")

---

### Moment 4: Sessions 2–4

**What the learner wants:**
- To try things at home and report back ("I texted my daughter!")
- To learn something new that builds on last week
- To practice in a safe place before trying alone
- To feel the tutor remembers what they struggled with
- To see progress (this week easier than last week)

**Anxieties present:**
- "What if I forgot everything I learned in session 1?"
- "What if the tutor makes me do something harder and I get lost?"
- "What if I can't practice at home because I'm tired/confused?"

**What the service does:**
- Tutor opens with: "Tell me what you practiced. How did it go?" (no judgment if the answer is "nothing")
- Tutor brings the handwritten reminder from last week and checks it together
- Tutor introduces one new skill that connects to the old one (not a random new thing)
- Learner practices the new skill for 45 minutes while tutor narrates
- Then learner practices alone (tutor sits back) for 20 minutes
- Tutor and learner talk about what's next: "Next week you could try... or we could do more of this?"
- Tutor writes a new reminder card

**Emotional outcome should be:**
- Momentum ("I'm getting better each week")
- Agency ("I get to choose what's next")
- Belonging ("This is my time each week and my tutor knows me")

---

### Moment 5: Goal Achievement and Beyond

**What the learner wants:**
- To be told they've done it (achieved their goal)
- To celebrate with other people who have done this
- To know what to do when the tutor is not there
- To feel they can still get help if they get stuck
- To know they can come back if they want more

**Anxieties present:**
- "What if I forget everything when I don't have my tutor?"
- "Will anyone want to see me again, or are we done?"

**What the service does:**
- Coordinator calls and says: "You came to learn [goal]. Can you do that now? I'm proud of you"
- Coordinator shares a one-page practice guide (three concrete ideas, not overwhelming)
- Learner is invited to a monthly tea and cake event (other learners and tutors there)
- Learner gets an optional follow-up postcard four weeks later: "How's your practice going?"
- Learner is told: "You can come to drop-in practice sessions anytime" (social, peer-led, no tutor)
- Learner has the tutor's phone number if they get stuck

**Emotional outcome should be:**
- Achievement ("I did it")
- Continuity ("This doesn't end, I can keep going")
- Community ("I'm part of a group of people learning this")
- Independence ("I can do this myself now")

## 3. Volunteer Tutor Onboarding Flow

### Step 1: Recruitment Message and Channel

**What happens:**
A volunteer sees a recruitment message through one or more of these channels:
- Library flyer or poster (placed in the library, community centres, GP surgeries): "Volunteer as a Smartphone Tutor — Help older adults learn to text, call, and connect. Four hours per month. No teaching experience needed."
- Local radio advert (monthly, 30 seconds): "Community Radio — Are you patient? Do you know how to use a smartphone? We're looking for volunteers to teach older adults in your local library. Call [number]."
- Word-of-mouth from a friend: "They're looking for tutors. I'm thinking of volunteering."
- Library website or newsletter: "Become a Smartphone Tutor"

The message is always the same: "No teaching experience needed," "Patient," "Help people stay connected with family," "Four hours per month," "Free training," "We'll match you with a learner."

#### Coordinator action:
The coordinator collects the volunteer's contact details (name, phone, email, availability) and schedules a brief intake call within three days.

#### Volunteer receives:
- Confirmation that we got their details
- A date and time for the intake call
- A one-page overview of what tutoring involves (optional, to read before the call)

---

### Step 2: Intake (Questions to Ask)

**What happens:**
The coordinator calls the volunteer at the scheduled time. The call takes 20 minutes. The coordinator reads from a script:

"Hi [name], thank you for volunteering with us. I'd like to ask you a few questions to make sure this is a good fit. Feel free to say no to anything — this is exploratory.

1. Can you tell me about your experience with smartphones? What can you do on yours? What do you find easy? Have you taught anyone before?
[Listen for: confidence with basic functions, patience, experience with different generations]

2. This tutoring is for older adults, aged 65 and above. Some have never used a phone before. How would you feel teaching someone who's completely new to this?
[Listen for: genuine warmth, no impatience, realistic expectations]

3. We ask tutors to come to the library once a week for four weeks with the same learner. Can you commit to that? What time works best for you?
[Listen for: stability, realism about schedule]

4. We'll give you a script and a session plan. How do you feel about following a plan closely?
[Listen for: flexibility, ability to learn a method, not ego-driven]

5. Is there anything that might hold you back from coming back for more learners after the first four weeks?
[Listen for: honesty about barriers, willingness to talk about concerns]

Thank you. I'm going to send you a training date and some reading materials."

#### Coordinator action:
After the call, the coordinator:
- Notes: confidence level, clarity of communication, any red flags (impatience, unclear availability, lack of empathy)
- If red flags, politely declines: "Thank you for offering. We'll keep your details and be in touch if we need tutors with different availability"
- If green lights, sends training date and pre-reading materials (see Step 3)

#### Volunteer receives:
- Confirmation they're approved for training
- Training date, time, and location
- Two pages of pre-reading: "What is the Smartphone Tutoring Programme?" (plain language overview) and "The Five Principles of Adult Learning" (see Design Tokens section for language register)

---

### Step 3: Training Session (2–3 hours, Scripted Outline)

**What happens:**
The training session is held at the library or a community centre, once per month or as needed. The coordinator or a trained senior tutor facilitates. All new tutors attend together (3–6 people at a time).

**Structure and content:**

**Welcome and icebreaker (15 minutes)**
Facilitator says: "Hi everyone, I'm [name]. We're here because you want to help older adults learn to use smartphones. Thank you. Before we start, let's go around the room. Can you say your name and one reason you're here?"
[Listening, not correcting]

**The Programme Overview (15 minutes)**
Facilitator presents:
- "The learners in our programme are 65 and above. Many have never held a smartphone. Many are worried they'll break it or mess up. Our job is to show them they can do this."
- "We work one-to-one. One learner, one tutor, four sessions over four weeks. First session is three hours, others are 90 minutes."
- "The learner sets the goal. We help them achieve it. We're not teaching them everything — just the thing they want to learn."
- [Show two example goals: "Text my grandson," "Video call my daughter in Australia"]
- "The learner does most of the doing. We narrate, then step back."

**The Tutor's Role and What You're NOT (15 minutes)**
Facilitator says:
- "You are a patient guide, not a teacher. You're not an expert (though you know phones). You're not here to judge. You're here to sit beside them and help them learn."
- "You will not grab the phone to fix their mistakes. You will not rush. You will not use jargon. You will not make them feel stupid."
- "You will celebrate every small success."
- [Give three examples of what to say and what not to say]
  - SAY: "Let me show you where that button is" INSTEAD OF: "Just click here"
  - SAY: "Let's try that again" INSTEAD OF: "No, you did it wrong"
  - SAY: "You're doing really well" INSTEAD OF: "This is easy"

**Plain Language Guide (20 minutes)**
Facilitator hands out a one-page "Language Guide" (printed, laminated card to keep):

| Avoid | Use | Why |
|-------|-----|-----|
| Click, swipe, tap, drag | Gentle press, slide your finger | "Tap" confuses people — they might hit harder |
| App | A programme or tool | "App" is jargon |
| WiFi, Bluetooth, password | Internet connection, numbers and letters you type in | Be specific, not cryptic |
| Status bar, notification, download | The bar at the top, a message that pops up, saved to your phone | Describe what it looks like |
| Pinch, zoom, gesture | Make smaller/bigger, enlarge | Use action words |
| Folder, shortcut, home screen | A place where apps live, a quick way to get there, the first screen | Concrete, not technical |
| Home button, back button | The button at the bottom, the arrow | Point while you say it |
| Charging port, USB cable | The hole where the cable goes, the cord | Describe the function |
| Charge, battery | "Plug it in," "The phone needs power" | Simpler verb phrase |

Facilitator says: "These are habits. You know this language, but the learner doesn't. Print this out, keep it on your phone, and when you catch yourself saying 'swipe,' just pause and try again."

**Session Structure and Script (45 minutes)**
Facilitator walks through the session outline (Session One and Sessions 2–4, see Section 4). Facilitator reads the tutor script out loud while volunteers follow along on a printed sheet. Facilitator pauses after each section and asks: "Any questions? Does this feel manageable?"

Key points covered:
- Opening (20 min): Welcome, goal elicitation. Script says: "Hi [name]. I'm [tutor name]. I'm really glad you're here. Let me tell you how today works, and then I want to hear what you want to learn."
- Device orientation (15 min): Show the parts, let them hold it, no pressure.
- First task (60 min): Narrator model, then learner does it.
- Break (15 min): Normal, not awkward.
- Second task or reinforcement (45 min): Build on the first, or deepen it.
- Reflection and planning (15 min): What worked? What to practice? When we meet next?

Facilitator emphasises: "Follow this structure. It's designed so the learner doesn't feel lost. The timing gives you permission to slow down. You're not here to cover a lot — you're here to help them do one thing well."

**Handling Nerves and Difficult Moments (20 minutes)**
Facilitator says: "You will meet a learner who is nervous, confused, or sad. Here's what to do:"

[Four scenarios with tutor response]

**Scenario 1: Learner is quiet and seems nervous**
- Tutor response: "This is new and a bit scary — that's completely normal. I was nervous the first time I used a phone too. We'll go slow. Nothing you can do will break anything. Let's start with just holding the phone."
- Why: Normalise, reassure, shrink the task

**Scenario 2: Learner can't remember something from last session**
- Tutor response: "That's totally normal. Our brains don't keep everything the first time. Let me show you again, and I'll write it down for you."
- Why: No shame, provide the reminder card

**Scenario 3: Learner says "I'm too old for this" or "I'll never get it"**
- Tutor response: "I've heard that from everyone I've worked with. You're learning something new — your brain is busy. That's not a sign you can't do it; it's a sign it's new. Let's try again."
- Why: Validate, reframe, build confidence

**Scenario 4: Learner does something and says "Did I break it?"**
- Tutor response: "No, you're fine. You can't break a phone by pressing buttons. The worst that happens is we turn it off and start again. You're in control."
- Why: Safety, agency, permission to explore

**Feedback Forms and Tutor Notes (10 minutes)**
Facilitator hands out the one-page tutor feedback form (see Coordinator Protocols section). Facilitator explains:
- "After each session, you fill this out. It takes five minutes. It helps the coordinator see how your learner is progressing. It also helps you remember what to do next time."
- "Write clearly. If you're not sure about something (e.g., 'Is the learner ready for a new task?'), note it and the coordinator will help."

**Next Steps and Logistics (10 minutes)**
Facilitator says: "After today, here's what happens: (1) You'll get an email with your first learner's name, goal, and first session date. (2) You'll call the learner to introduce yourself — I'll give you a script. (3) You'll review the session outline the night before. (4) You'll come 30 minutes early to set up. (5) You'll have the session. (6) You'll fill out the feedback form. (7) You'll come back next week and do it again. Any questions?"

**Closing (10 minutes)**
Facilitator says: "Thank you for being here. This is important work. You're going to help someone feel less alone and more connected. If you get stuck or confused, you can always email me or call. I'm here to support you. Welcome to the team."

[Hand out printed materials pack: Language Guide, Session One outline, Sessions 2–4 outline, Tutor introduction script, Feedback form, Coordinator's phone number and email, Certificate of Completion]

#### Volunteer receives:
- Printed materials pack (session outlines, language guide, feedback form, scripts)
- Certificate of completion
- Coordinator's contact details
- Confirmation of first learner match (within one week)

## 4. Session Structure: Two Templates with Tutor Scripts

### Session One (3 hours total)

**Purpose:** Build trust, understand the learner's goal, introduce the device, begin the first task, plan home practice.

**Timing:**
- Welcome and goal elicitation (20 min)
- Device orientation (15 min)
- First task, learner-driven (60 min)
- Break (15 min)
- Second task or reinforcement (45 min)
- Reflection and planning (15 min)
- Total: 180 minutes

---

#### Opening: Welcome and Goal Elicitation (20 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Arrive 30 minutes early. Test the phone, check WiFi, settle yourself.
- Greet the learner by name at the door. Smile. Offer tea or water.
- Sit next to the learner, not across a table.
- Place a notebook and pen on the table between you.

**Tutor script — read this aloud (adapt only names and goals):**

"Hi [learner name], I'm so glad you're here. I'm [tutor name]. Thank you for coming. I know learning something new can feel a bit daunting, so before we start, let me tell you how today is going to work.

We're going to spend three hours together today. You're going to hold the phone a lot — I want you to get comfortable with it. I'm going to show you how to do things, and then you're going to try. There's no test. There's no rush. If something feels tricky, we'll slow down or try again. There's no way to break a phone by pressing buttons, so you're safe to explore.

The most important part of today is that I want to understand what you want to learn. You said you want to [their goal — e.g., 'video call your daughter']. Let me hear more about that.

Tell me: Who do you want to [call/text/photograph/connect with]? What would it mean to you if you could do this? Have you tried before, and if so, what happened?"

[Listen. Write down key words. Reflect back: "So you want to video call your daughter in Melbourne every Sunday — is that right?"]

"Great. Today we're going to work on that. I'm not going to teach you everything about phones — just the thing you want to do. By the end of today, you'll be able to [goal], or very close to it. And then we'll meet three more times to practice and deepen what you've learned.

Does that sound okay? Any questions before we start?"

[If learner is nervous]: "I know this might feel scary. That's completely normal. Everyone I've worked with has felt a bit nervous at the start. By the end of the day, you'll feel differently. I promise."

**Outcome:** Learner feels welcomed, understood, and safe. Learner knows the plan for the day.

---

#### Device Orientation (15 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Hand the phone to the learner. Let them hold it. Do not grab it back.
- Narrate every single thing you do.
- Use the Language Guide (plain language, no jargon).
- Go slowly.

**Tutor script:**

"This is the phone. It's called a smartphone — 'smart' because it can think and learn. Hold it. Get a feel for it. It's not fragile — you won't hurt it.

Here's the screen — the flat part in the front. That's what we see and touch.

At the bottom, there's a button — can you feel it? That's the on/off button. It's here [point and press slowly]. Watch. When I press it, the screen lights up.

See the screen is now on. You can see lots of little pictures — these are called 'apps' or 'programmes.' Each one does a different thing. For example, this little green one with a phone symbol is how you make calls.

Now, let me show you how to unlock the phone so we can use it. I'm going to slide my finger up from the bottom. Watch. [Do it slowly.] Now the phone is unlocked. You try it. Go ahead, slide your finger up from the bottom. [Guide their hand if needed.] Great — you did it!

Now the phone is ready. You see all the little pictures. These are the apps or programmes we'll use today.

Let me show you the pieces. At the top of the phone, there's a speaker — that's where sound comes out. At the bottom, there's a speaker too. The little hole at the bottom is where the charger plugs in — we'll do that later. The button on the side is the volume button — it makes sound louder or quieter.

This back part is a camera — you can take photos with this.

Does this feel okay? Any questions? Good. You're doing really well."

**Outcome:** Learner has held the phone, knows the basic parts, and has unlocked it themselves. Learner feels they can touch the phone without fear.

---

#### First Task, Learner-Driven (60 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Choose the learner's goal (e.g., "video call your daughter").
- Show the task once, slowly, narrating every step.
- Then hand the phone to the learner and let them do it.
- Narrate what you see them doing ("You're pressing the green button — good").
- Do not take the phone to fix their mistakes. If they get lost, narrate the next step and let them do it.
- Celebrate effort, not just success.

**Tutor script for "Making a Phone Call" (adapt for other goals):**

"Now I'm going to show you how to make a phone call. Let's call your daughter [daughter's name]. I'm going to show you once slowly, and then you're going to try.

Watch. The green app with a phone symbol — that's for making calls. I'm going to tap it. [Do it slowly.] Now I see a screen with a pad of numbers, like a telephone keypad you might remember. I'm going to tap the numbers for your daughter's phone number. Her number is [read it out loud]. Watch me do it once.

[Tap each number slowly and aloud]: 0... 3... 9... 7... 5... 2... 1... 0... 0... [Full number]. 

Now I see her number on the screen. To make the call, I press the big green button at the bottom. Watch. [Press it.] Now the phone is calling. You can hear it ringing. I'm going to wait for her to answer. [Pause. If she answers, say hello and hang up gently. If it rings out, say 'She's not answering right now — that's okay. The call ended.']

Now you're going to try. I'm going to be right here. The green phone app is already open on the screen. You're going to tap the numbers for [another person's number — perhaps someone else in their life, or a test number]. Are you ready?

[Hand the phone to the learner.]

Okay, go ahead. Press the green app... oh wait, it's already open. Good. Now, the first number is [number]. Can you find that number? [Wait while they look.] Yes, there it is. Tap it. [They do.] Good. Now the next number is [number]. [Continue through all numbers.]

Now you have the full number on the screen. To call them, press the big green button at the bottom. [They do.] Great! The phone is calling. Listen — you can hear it ringing.

[After a few rings or a connection, say:] Perfect. You just made a phone call. You did that."

[If they made a mistake, rewind calmly]: "Oops, that's okay. You tapped the wrong number. No problem — let me show you what to do. See this button [point]? If you press it, it deletes that number. Go ahead, press it. Good. Now let's try the number again."

[Continue for 60 minutes, with the learner doing most of the work. If they finish the main task early, introduce a related skill: "You've made a call. Now let me show you how to save her number so you don't have to type it in next time."]

**Outcome:** Learner has made (or attempted) a real phone call. Learner knows they can do it. Learner is tired but proud.

---

#### Break (15 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Stop. Step back. Normal conversation.
- Offer tea, coffee, water, biscuit.
- Chat about anything but the phone ("How long have you lived here?" "What do you like to do?").
- Let the learner's brain rest.

**Tutor script:**

"You've done a lot in the last hour. Let's take a break. Can I get you a tea? [Offer.] Let's sit for a bit. [Chat.]"

**Outcome:** Learner feels cared for. Learner is refreshed for the second half.

---

#### Second Task or Reinforcement (45 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Either: Repeat the first task so the learner feels confident (if they struggled), or introduce a new, related skill (if they flew through it).
- Use the same narration + learner-does model.

**Tutor script (reinforcing the first task — "making a second call"):**

"Now we've had a break. Let me show you how to make another call. This time, you're going to call [another person they want to contact — perhaps a family member, friend, or number you've agreed on]. The steps are exactly the same as before.

Remember: green app, tap the numbers, press the green button. You've done this once — you know how.

[Hand the phone.]

Go ahead. Which number are you calling? [They tell you.] Good. Open the green phone app. [They do or you guide them.] Now tap the numbers. [They do.] Great. Now press the green button. [They do.] Beautiful — you're calling again."

**Tutor script (new related skill — "saving a contact"):**

"You've made phone calls. Now I want to show you something that will make it even easier next time. Instead of typing the numbers every time, you can save the number to your phone so it remembers it.

Watch me do it once. I'm going to open the green app again [do it]. Now, instead of typing numbers, I'm going to look for a button that says 'Add' or 'Plus' or has a + symbol. There it is [point]. I'm going to tap it. [Do it.] Now the phone is asking me for a name. I'm going to type the name — let me type [contact name]. [Type it slowly.] Now I'm going to tap the number field and type the number [type slowly]. Now I press 'Save' [point and press]. And now the phone remembers this person.

You try. You're going to save [another contact's] number. I'll help. Go ahead — open the green app. [They do.] Now look for the + or Add button. Can you see it? [Guide if needed.] Tap it. [They do.] Now it's asking for a name. Type [name]. [They do, slowly.] Good. Now tap the number field and type the number [number]. [They do.] Perfect. Now press Save. [They do.] Fantastic — you've saved a contact. Next time you want to call them, you just find their name — you don't have to type the number."

[Repeat this task once more so they feel confident.]

**Outcome:** Learner has either reinforced the first skill and feels confident, or learned a new but related skill. Learner is leaving with two things they can do.

---

#### Reflection and Planning (15 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Recap what they learned.
- Ask them to show you (so they practice again).
- Discuss what to practice at home.
- Write a reminder card by hand, slowly, while talking through it.
- Confirm the next session date.

**Tutor script:**

"Before we finish, let me ask you: what have you learned today?

[Listen. They should say something like: 'I made phone calls' or 'I learned how to save a number.']

Perfect. Let me ask you to show me one more time. Can you [original task — make a call or save a contact]? [They do it, with you narrating so they hear it again.] Excellent. You've got this.

Now, for the next two weeks, I want you to practice at home. Here's what I'm going to suggest: [Choose one realistic task based on what they learned.]

For example: 'Call your daughter every other day. It doesn't have to be long — just say hello.'

Or: 'Take three photos and show them to someone.'

Or: 'Find and tap on your daughter's name to call her' [if they saved the contact].

What feels realistic to you? What could you actually do? [Listen and agree on something they genuinely might do.]

[Write on a card in large letters (18pt+), slowly, while saying:]

'Practice this week: Call [person] using the green phone app. Or tap [person's] name if you saved it.'

'Date: [Today's date]'

'Your tutor: [Your name]'

'Next session: [Day and date]'

This card is yours. Keep it somewhere you'll see it — by your bed, in your handbag, on the fridge. Look at it and try to practice a few times this week. If you get stuck, call me. [Give your phone number again.] Any questions?"

[If they don't practice, that's fine. Next session, you won't judge. You'll just say: "Did you practice?" and whatever they say, you'll move on.]

**Outcome:** Learner leaves with a clear memory of what they learned, a written reminder, and one realistic task to practice. Learner has the tutor's phone number. Learner knows the next session date.

---

### Sessions 2–4 (90 minutes total)

**Purpose:** Recap and check home practice, learn a new skill or deepen the old one, practice independently, plan next steps.

**Timing:**
- Recap and home practice review (15 min)
- New task or reinforcement (45 min)
- Independent practice (20 min)
- Next-steps planning (10 min)
- Total: 90 minutes

---

#### Recap and Home Practice Review (15 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Greet warmly by name.
- Bring the reminder card from last week.
- Ask about their practice without judgment.
- Reflect what they say back to them.

**Tutor script:**

"Hi [name], it's so good to see you. How have you been? [Chat.] Last time we met, we worked on [skill]. Do you remember? [Show the reminder card.] Let me ask — did you practice this week? Did you try to [task]?

[Listen. They may say 'Yes, I called my daughter three times,' or 'No, I forgot,' or 'I tried once.' None of this is wrong.]

[Reflect back]: 'So you called your daughter once. That's great. Did it feel easier than the first time?'

Or: 'So you didn't get a chance to practice. That's totally okay. We all have busy weeks. But I'm glad you're here. Let's jump in and we'll practice together.'

[If they practiced]: 'I'm proud of you. You kept at it. How did it feel? Did you feel more confident?'

[If they want to show you]: 'Show me. Go ahead — open the phone and call [person].' [They do, with you narrating so they hear it again.]"

**Outcome:** Learner feels welcomed and un-judged whether they practiced or not. Learner revisits the skill from last week. Tutor assesses readiness for new learning.

---

#### New Task or Reinforcement (45 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- If learner felt shaky last week (confidence 1–3), repeat the task but with a small variation.
- If learner felt confident last week (confidence 4–5), introduce a new, related skill.
- Use the same model: tutor narrates, then learner does.

**Tutor script (reinforcement — "Making calls to saved contacts"):**

"Last week you learned to call and to save a contact. This week, let's put them together. You saved your daughter's number. Now let me show you how to call her using that saved number — it's even easier because you don't have to type the number.

Watch. I open the phone. Then I open the green phone app [do it]. Now, instead of the number pad, I look for an option that says 'Contacts' or shows a picture of people. There it is [point]. I tap it. [Do it.] Now I see a list of saved contacts. I scroll down — that means I slide my finger down the list. [Do it slowly.] I'm looking for [person]. There — [person]. I tap their name. [Tap it.] Now their number appears, and I press the green button to call. [Press.] That's it — a call to a saved contact is the same as a regular call, but easier because you don't have to remember the number.

Your turn. You have [contact] saved. Open the phone. [They do.] Open the green app. [They do.] Find the Contacts button. [They do or you guide them.] Tap it. [They do.] Now scroll down to find [contact]. [They do.] Tap their name. [They do.] Perfect. Now press the green button. [They do.] Fantastic — you just called a saved contact. That's easier, right?"

[Do this twice so they feel confident with the new step of scrolling.]

**Tutor script (new skill — "Sending a text message"):**

"Last week you learned phone calls. This week I'm going to teach you something different: sending a text message. A text message is a short message you type and send to someone. It's like a letter, but it arrives in seconds.

Let me show you. [Open the phone.] I look for a green app with a little speech bubble or message symbol. There it is. I tap it. [Tap it.] Now it's asking me who I want to text. I look for the option to start a new message. There — it says 'New Message' or has a + symbol. I tap it. [Tap it.] Now it's asking me for a phone number or contact name. Let me type [contact name]. [Type.] Now there's a space where I can type my message. I'm going to type: 'Hi [contact], it's [learner name]. I'm learning to use my phone! How are you?' [Type it slowly, showing each letter.] Now I look for a Send button — usually green, with an arrow or the word 'Send.' [Find it.] I tap it. [Tap it.] And the message is sent. The other person will get it in their phone in a few seconds.

That's texting. You try. I'll stay with you. Open your phone. [They do.] Find the message app — the one with the speech bubble. [They do or you guide.] Tap it. [They do.] Now find 'New Message.' [They do.] Type [contact name]. [They do.] Now, in the message space, type: 'Hi [contact], I'm learning to text!' [They type slowly.] Now find the Send button. [They find it.] Tap it. [They do.] Excellent. You just sent a text message. You did it!"

[Do this twice with different messages.]

**Outcome:** Learner has reinforced the previous skill or learned a new, related skill. Learner has done the skill at least twice and feels more confident.

---

#### Independent Practice (20 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Step back. Sit across the room or nearby but quiet.
- Give the learner a task: "Call [person]" or "Text [person] to say hello" or "Take a photo of something in this room and show me."
- Do not narrate. Do not hover. Do not jump in unless asked.
- The learner fumbles, hesitates, maybe gets a bit lost. That's normal. Wait.
- If they ask for help, guide them: "What did we do first? That's right — open the green app."
- If they succeed, celebrate: "You did that all by yourself. That's fantastic."

**Tutor script:**

"Now I'm going to sit here and let you practice on your own. I want to see you do this without me narrating. You're going to [task — call, text, take a photo]. Go ahead. I'm right here if you need me, but see if you can do this yourself.

[Sit back. Stay quiet. Watch. If they ask: "What was the next step?" — answer with a question: "What did we do first?" If they say "I don't know" — remind them in one sentence: "We opened the green app and found the person. Try that."

When they finish the task]: 'You did that all by yourself. You should be proud. That's what learning looks like.'"

**Outcome:** Learner practices without the tutor's narration and discovers they can remember the steps. Learner gains independence and confidence.

---

#### Next-Steps Planning (10 minutes)

**Tutor does:**
- Ask what they want to learn next.
- Confirm the next session date.
- Write a reminder card for home practice.

**Tutor script:**

"You've learned a lot. You can now [skill from this session]. Next time we meet, we could learn [logical next skill], or we could go deeper with [current skill]. What would you like to do?

[Listen and agree.]

Great. For the next week, I want you to practice [task]. [Write it on a card in large letters, slowly, while saying it aloud.] Keep this somewhere you'll see it. And I'll see you [day and date] at [time].

Any questions? You're doing so well. I'm looking forward to seeing what you learn next."

**Outcome:** Learner leaves with a clear next step, a written reminder, and confidence that they're progressing.

## 5. Key Touchpoints: Coordinator Protocols

### Protocol 1: Booking Confirmation Call

**Timing:** Within 2 working days of enquiry.

### Protocol 4: Absence Follow-Up Call

**Timing:** Within 48 hours of a missed session.

**Purpose:** Check the learner is okay, understand what happened, reschedule without shame or blame.

**Duration:** 5–8 minutes.

**When this is used:**
- Learner does not show up to a scheduled session and cannot be reached before or during the session.

**Coordinator script:**

"Hi [learner name], this is [coordinator name] from the library. I'm ringing to check you're okay. You had a session with [tutor name] on [day] at [time], and we didn't see you. Is everything all right?"

[If someone answers and learner is unwell, unable to speak, etc.:]
"I'm sorry to hear that. Please let [learner name] know we hope they feel better soon. No pressure at all — when they're ready, just call us and we'll reschedule. Here's our number: [number]."

[If learner answers:]
"I'm so pleased you're okay. What happened? [Listen without judgment.] I understand. That sounds like a lot to deal with. [Name], I want you to know there's no trouble. Life gets in the way sometimes.

Are you still interested in learning to [goal]? [Listen.] Great. Let me reschedule you. What day and time would suit you better? [Offer three options.] Which works? [Confirm.] Perfect.

Your new session is [day] at [time]. [Tutor name] is available then. I'll send you a new letter with the details. And we'll call to remind you the day before.

You're not in trouble at all. Just come when you're ready. We're here to help."

[If learner says they want to stop:] "I'm sorry to hear that. Can I ask — is there something we could do differently to make this work for you? [Listen.] Okay, I understand. If you change your mind, we'd love to have you back. Just ring us. [Number.]"

[If learner says they can't be reached:] "No problem. When you're ready to reschedule, please give us a call on [number] and ask for [coordinator name]. We'll sort it out."

[End:] "Thank you. I hope to see you soon. Goodbye."

**What the coordinator does after the call:**

- If learner wants to continue, immediately reschedules and notifies tutor.
- Sends new confirmation letter.
- Notes in tracking spreadsheet: "Absence on [date]. Reason: [reason]. Rescheduled to [new date]."
- Sends new reminder 48 hours before the rescheduled session.
- If learner says they want to stop, thanks them and makes a note: "Learner [name] discontinued on [date]. Reason: [reason]. Open to return."

**Materials:**

No special materials for this protocol — uses standard confirmation letter (Protocol 1) when rescheduling.

### Protocol 5: Tutor Post-Session Feedback Form

**Timing:** Filled in by tutor within 2 hours of session end.

**Purpose:** Track learner progress, identify any barriers or breakthroughs, help coordinator support both tutor and learner, gather data to improve the programme.

**Format:** One-page form, simple language, not a test or evaluation of the tutor.

**Form template:**

[Library logo]

**Tutor Post-Session Feedback Form**

| Field | Instructions | Example |
|-------|--------------|---------|
| **Learner name** | First name only | Mary |
| **Session number** | Which session in the 4-session cycle? | Session 2 of 4 |
| **Date and time** | When did the session happen? | Thursday 14 Oct, 2–3:30pm |
| **How was [learner's] mood today? (1 sentence)** | Describe their emotional state. Were they relaxed, nervous, excited, tired? | Nervous at the start, relaxed and smiling by the end |
| **Did the learner complete the planned task?** | Did they do what you intended to teach? | Yes, they made two phone calls and saved a contact |
| **What specific skills can they do now that they couldn't at the start of today's session? (1–2 sentences)** | Be concrete. "Make a phone call" not "better at phones." | They can hold a phone without hesitation. They can find the green phone app. They can tap numbers. |
| **Confidence level (1–5)** | 1 = very nervous, 5 = very confident. Rate their confidence in the skill we focused on. | 3 (was 1 or 2, improving) |
| **What will the learner practice at home? (1 sentence)** | What specific task did you ask them to practice? | Call their daughter using the saved contact every other day |
| **Did anything confuse them? Did you have to repeat anything? (1 sentence)** | Note any tricky moments. This helps the coordinator and helps you plan next session. | They were confused about where the Save button is. We repeated it twice. They got it. |
| **Anything else the coordinator should know? (1 sentence, optional)** | Anything relevant to their learning, wellbeing, or context? | Learner's daughter is visiting next week. They want to be able to video call her by then. Is that feasible in session 3? |

**Coordinator's actions after receiving the form:**

1. **Flag patterns:** If the learner is confused about the same thing for two sessions, the coordinator reaches out to the tutor: "I've seen that passwords came up in sessions 1 and 2. Would you like to troubleshoot that together before session 3?"

2. **Adjust if needed:** If the learner's confidence dropped, the coordinator might call the learner to check in (not to assess, but to support): "How are you finding it? Is there anything that's confusing?"

3. **Celebrate:** If a tutor notes a big breakthrough, the coordinator replies: "I loved reading that [learner] went from 1 to 3 in confidence. You're doing a great job."

4. **Data collection:** Once per quarter, the coordinator reviews all feedback forms to see: (a) how many learners achieve their goal fully, partially, or not yet; (b) what topics confuse learners most (e.g., passwords, scrolling, saving); (c) what tutors' most common challenges are; (d) whether there are patterns in who drops out and why.

## 6. Design Tokens for Printed Materials

### Rationale

Older adults (65+) experience age-related changes in vision, hearing, and cognition. This is not disability; it is normal aging. Printed materials must accommodate these changes so the learner can access information independently and feel respected. All token choices are evidence-based in accessibility research cited below.

---

### Type Sizes and Scale

**Minimum body text: 16pt**
- Older adults' eyes require more light and stronger contrast. WCAG 2.2 large text is 18pt+. We use 16pt minimum for body, knowing this is the threshold at which most older adults can read comfortably for extended periods.

**Recommended hierarchy:**

| Use case | Size | Weight | Line height | Example |
|----------|------|--------|------------|---------|
| Primary heading (e.g., letter opening) | 24pt | Bold | 1.5 | "Your Smartphone Tutoring Session" |
| Secondary heading (e.g., section subhead) | 20pt | Bold | 1.5 | "Your first session:" |
| Body text (main content) | 16pt | Regular | 1.5 | "Come 10 minutes early so you can settle in." |
| Small text (disclaimers, optional detail) | 14pt | Regular | 1.5 | "If you have questions, call [number]" |
| Reminder cards (handwritten) | 18pt+ | Large, clear handwriting | — | Practice reminder written by tutor |

**No text smaller than 14pt.** If content is important, make it 16pt. If it's optional, cut it.

---

### Contrast Ratios

**WCAG 2.2 AA standard (legally required as of January 2026 in US federal services, and July 2026 in Washington state):**

- Normal text (< 18pt): 4.5:1 contrast ratio minimum
- Large text (18pt+ or 14pt bold+): 3:1 contrast ratio minimum

**We exceed this for older adults:**

- Body text: 7:1 contrast (high contrast)
- Headings: 7:1 contrast
- Example: Black (hex #000000) on white (#FFFFFF) = 21:1 (excellent)

**Colours to use:**

| Element | Foreground (text) | Background | Hex | Contrast ratio | Notes |
|---------|-------------------|------------|-----|-----------------|-------|
| Body text, all fonts | Black | White | #000000 on #FFFFFF | 21:1 | Maximum contrast, easy to read |
| Heading, emphasis | Dark blue | White | #003366 on #FFFFFF | 11:1 | Professional, high contrast |
| Links (if used) | Dark blue (underlined) | White | #003366 on #FFFFFF | 11:1 | Underlined so colour-blind readers can tell it's clickable |
| Warning / important info | Dark red | White or cream | #660000 on #FFFEF0 | 9:1 | Attention without aggression |
| Background block (optional emphasis) | Black text | Pale cream | #000000 on #FFFEF0 | 18:1 | Warm, easy on eyes, distinct |

**Avoid:**
- Light grey text (hard to read)
- White text on pale backgrounds
- Red and green used together (colour-blind users can't distinguish)
- Small text in lower contrast

---

### Line Spacing

**Minimum 1.5 line height for all text.**

- Older adults need more space between lines to track from line to line.
- 1.5x is the WCAG 2.2 AA standard for body text.
- Use 1.5 minimum for 16pt body text.
- Use 1.5 for headings (more space helps readability).

**Example:**

```
Original (no line height):
Come 10 minutes early so you can settle in and have a cup of tea.

With 1.5 line height:
Come 10 minutes early so you can settle in
and have a cup of tea.
[visible space between lines]
```

---

### Language Register and Tone

**All printed materials use plain language (Plain English Campaign standard):**

| Avoid | Use | Why |
|-------|-----|-----|
| "Utilise the device" | "Use the phone" | Simpler verb |
| "Access the application" | "Open the app" or "Find the programme" | Concrete, shorter |
| "Configure your settings" | "Change your options" | Clearer action |
| "Smartphone" (repeated) | "Phone" | "Smartphone" is jargon; most older adults say "phone" |
| "Your tutor will facilitate learning" | "Your tutor will help you learn" | Active, warm |
| "Optimise your experience" | "Make it easier for you" | Direct benefit |

**Tone:**
- Warm, never condescending
- Direct, never cryptic
- Active verbs ("you will," "we will")
- Second person ("you") when addressing the learner
- No idioms ("hit the ground running," "ball in your court")
- No metaphors ("navigate," "journey," "onboarding") — use literal language
- Short sentences (under 15 words where possible)

**Example of wrong tone:**
"Our innovative mobile learning solution leverages peer-to-peer engagement paradigms to optimise geriatric digital literacy."

**Example of right tone:**
"We teach older adults to use smartphones. A volunteer tutor meets with you for four weeks. You learn the things that matter to you."

---

### Heading Hierarchy

Every printed document has a clear visual hierarchy:

1. **Document title (24pt bold)** — one per page
2. **Section headings (20pt bold)** — 1–3 per page
3. **Body text (16pt regular)** — main content
4. **Emphasis within body (16pt bold)** — highlights, not separate lines

**Do not skip levels.** Do not go from 24pt title straight to 14pt body.

### Avoid List (for all printed materials)

- **Jargon:** app, WiFi, Bluetooth, swipe, tap, click, gesture, notification, status bar, download, password, home screen, folder, shortcut, USB, charging port, icon, widget
- **Idioms:** "hits the ground running," "ball in your court," "learning curve," "get your head around," "wrap your head around," "at the end of the day"
- **Metaphors:** "navigate," "journey," "onboarding," "pain point," "user flow," "touchpoint," "friction," "leverage," "ecosystem"
- **Acronyms:** WiFi, SMS, PIN, QR (even with definition — use "code you can scan" instead), URL, HTTP, USB
- **Negatives (if possible):** Instead of "Don't worry about breaking it," say "You can't break it by pressing buttons"
- **Assumptions:** Do not assume learners know what a screen is, what a button is, or that they've held a phone before
- **Cultural references:** "Like your grandkid does," "as easy as texting your kids"
- **Conditional phrases:** Instead of "If you need to," say "When you want to"

---

### Layout and Visual Design Principles

1. **White space:** At least 20mm margin on all sides (not crowded).
2. **One column:** Never multi-column for body text. Single column is easier to follow.
3. **Alignment:** Left-aligned body text and headings (not centered, easier to track).
4. **Emphasis:** Use bold for headings and one key phrase per section. No italics (hard to read at small sizes). No underline (except for links).
5. **Graphics:** Use clear, simple line drawings or photos of real people (not cartoons, not silhouettes). Avoid small decorative elements.
6. **Paper:** Off-white or pale cream (not bright white, less harsh on eyes). Matte finish (not glossy, reduces glare).

## Accessibility Notes: Three Principles for Service Delivery

### 1. Conversation Over Assumption
Never assume what an older adult knows or doesn't know. Learner comes first — ask, listen, adapt. The tutor's opening question ("What do you want to learn?") is the most important part of the first session. It centres the learner's goal, not what the tutor thinks they should learn. This builds agency and motivation. If the learner struggles with something, it's not because they're old; it's because the instruction wasn't clear enough yet. The tutor's job is to find another way to explain, not to lower the expectation.

### 2. Accessibility as Standard, Not Add-On
All materials are designed with large print, high contrast, plain language, and handwritten reminders from the start. We don't have "accessible versions" and "regular versions" — we have one, accessible version. Every tutor has a language guide and sees it as non-negotiable (not as being nice, but as being effective). Every reminder card is handwritten in large letters by the tutor because the learner remembers it better that way. Every postcard and letter is 16pt minimum because that's the size that works. This is service design, not accommodation.

### 3. Community and Continuity Reduce Anxiety
Older adults often feel isolated or ashamed about not knowing how to use a phone. Anxiety blocks learning. We reduce anxiety through:
- **Consistency:** Same room, same time, same tutor for all four sessions. Predictability reduces stress.
- **Normalisation:** The tutor says aloud: "Everyone finds this tricky at first." "You're doing really well." Not empty praise, but genuine recognition of effort.
- **Social proof:** Learners hear about other older adults learning ("We've taught 40 people this year"). The monthly celebration event shows that many learners are part of this. Peer community is more powerful than tutor reassurance.
- **Handwritten touch:** Reminder cards and thank-you postcards are handwritten because they feel personal. A typed letter feels institutional; a handwritten card feels like someone cares.
- **Continuity after the programme:** The learner doesn't disappear after session 4. Optional drop-in practice, follow-up postcards, and the invitation to call their tutor all say: "You're still part of this." This sustains motivation and prevents the learner from sliding back.
