## Accessibility is Standard, Not Add-On

**Rule:** In service design for older adults, accessibility is built into every touchpoint from the start — one version for everyone, not "accessible versions" versus "regular versions."

**Why:** Labeling some materials as "accessible versions" creates stigma and implies the main version is the default. When large print, high contrast, plain language, and handwritten reminders are the only version available, they become the standard. Everyone benefits (younger users with low vision, users in bright light, users processing new information). The design becomes more usable for all ages.

**How to apply:** 
- All printed materials: 16pt minimum body text, 7:1 contrast (black on white standard), 1.5 line height
- All scripts and speech: Plain language (no jargon, no idioms, no metaphors), short sentences
- All reminders: Handwritten by hand when possible (reminder cards, thank-you notes, follow-up postcards)
- Measure: If you're creating a separate "large print version," stop. Make that the only version.

## Conversation Over Assumption

**Rule:** Never assume what an older adult knows or doesn't know. Ask, listen, adapt. Learner's goal comes first, not tutor's curriculum.

**Why:** Older adults have lived 60+ years and often feel infantilised when someone assumes they're incompetent because they're new to smartphones. When the tutor's first action is to listen ("What do you want to learn?"), it centres agency and respect. When something confuses the learner, it's not because they're old — it's because the instruction wasn't clear enough yet. The tutor's job is to find another way to explain.

**How to apply:**
- Open every session with the learner's goal, not the curriculum
- If they struggle: "Let me show you a different way" (not "Let me simplify it")
- If they plateau: Ask ("What would help right now?") instead of deciding for them
- Recognition language: "You're doing really well" (specific, honest effort) not "This is easy" (dismissive, implies anyone should get it)

## Handwritten Touch in Digital Services

**Rule:** In digital literacy programmes, handwritten elements (reminder cards, thank-you notes, follow-up postcards) are more effective than typed templates because they signal personal care and are more memorable.

**Why:** Older adults who are learning digital skills often feel alienated from technology. A handwritten reminder card written by their tutor ("Practice calling your daughter every other day — Michael") feels personal. A typed postcard feels institutional. The handwritten element also anchors the memory — they look at the card, see the familiar handwriting, and remember what to practice.

**How to apply:**
- Tutor writes reminder cards by hand in 18pt+ after each session
- Coordinator writes thank-you postcards by hand within 24 hours
- Follow-up check-in postcards are also handwritten or at least personalized with the learner's name and one detail from their goal
- If the learner keeps these cards, they become a tangible reminder of progress

## Community and Continuity Reduce Anxiety

**Rule:** Older adults learning digital skills often experience shame or isolation. Anxiety blocks learning. Build in social proof, normalisation, and continuity.

**Why:** "I'm too old" or "Everyone else gets this" are psychological barriers, not cognitive ones. When a learner hears that 40 others have learned in the past year, sees peers at a celebration event, and receives follow-up check-ins after the programme, their anxiety drops and they're more likely to keep practicing alone.

**How to apply:**
- Consistency: Same room, time, tutor for all four sessions (predictability reduces stress)
- Normalisation: Tutor says aloud "Everyone finds this tricky at first"
- Social proof: Tell learners about other learners ("We've taught 40 people")
- Celebration event: Monthly gathering with other learners and tutors (peer community is more powerful than tutor reassurance)
- Continuity: Optional drop-in practice, follow-up postcards, tutor's phone number available, keeps learner feeling supported after formal sessions end
