Honest uncertainty builds trust

Users report higher trust when the navigation companion admits uncertainty or owns its mistakes, while repeated errors or lack of honest cues reduce trust.

Limit: The evidence comes from a limited number of interview transcripts, so it may not represent broader user populations.

Honestly I only trust the companion when it admits it is unsure. It once said it could not find the lab and asked me to look for a sign, and that made me trust it more.
Transcript 2
A good companion speaks rarely, shows the next step only, and owns its mistakes. Trust is built less by accuracy than by honest uncertainty.
Text 1
But when it gave me the wrong turn twice I stopped believing it. I wish I could switch off the chatter in quiet places like the reading room.
Transcript 3

Cognitive load from visual and auditory clutter

Frequent visual overlays and excessive voice instructions distract users, causing them to look away, stop listening, or dismiss the companion.

Limit: The evidence comes only from a small number of self‑reported experiences and does not include objective measures of cognitive load.

Navigation aids in mixed reality fail when they compete for attention. Designers keep adding overlays, but pedestrians need a calm view of the real street.
Text 1
The first time [participant] showed me the companion, it talked too much and I stopped listening.
Transcript 1
Too many pop-ups pull my attention away from the corridor and I nearly walked into a glass door.
Transcript 2
One participant stopped mid-corridor to dismiss a notification.
Text 2

Social visibility and physical discomfort

Wearing a heavy mixed‑reality headset in public makes users feel self‑conscious because people stare, and it can cause physical discomfort such as dizziness.

Limit: The evidence comes from a limited set of participant comments and cannot indicate how common these experiences are across a broader population.

[participant] from my hostel said the glasses made him dizzy. People stare when I speak to it, so I mostly use the arrows.
Transcript 3
Wearing a heavy headset in public also carries a social cost, because people react to what they cannot see.
Text 1
I would feel silly talking to a companion in front of other people. At home it feels fine, in a crowded lobby it does not.
Transcript 2

Modality preference and readability

Participants prefer glanceable arrow cues over voice instructions, and they experience visual fatigue from small text and physical fatigue from wearing the headset for extended periods.

Limit: The findings are based on a small pilot study with only a few participants, limiting generalizability.

I wear the headset when I walk through a new campus building because the arrows on the floor save me from stopping every minute.
Transcript 1
In the pilot, two of three participants returned to arrows after the voice mode misheard place names. Observers saw frequent glances at the floor and at phones.
Text 2
[participant] told me that voice instructions work better for her, but I prefer arrows because I can glance at them. The device is heavy after twenty minutes.
Transcript 2
After a long walk my eyes get tired and the small text is hard to read.
Transcript 1

Companion as social anchor

The navigation companion reduces feelings of isolation by greeting users by name and offering a sense of companionship during exploration.

Limit: The evidence is based only on a few transcript excerpts, so it may not represent broader user experiences.

As a new student I felt isolated in the first week and the companion gave me a reason to explore. It remembered where the library was and greeted me by name, which felt friendly.
Transcript 3
Sometimes I forget I am alone in the corridor, and that feels good. My friend [participant] asked me to write to [email] or call [phone] if I want to try it again.
